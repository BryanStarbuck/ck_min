# **INTRODUCTION** 

Defendant, Tyler James Robinson (“Mr. Robinson”), by and through undersigned counsel, hereby files his response to the State’s _Motion to Amend Or Clarify the Pre-Trial and Trial Publicity Order_ (hereinafter “the motion to amend”). Dkt. No. 167. As explained below, Mr. Robinson does not agree that the Court’s Protection Order Re: Pre-Trial and Trial Publicity (“Publicity Order”) is overbroad or “vague for voidness” as the State alleges, nor does it improperly balance Mr. Robinson’s unqualified Fifth and Eighth Amendment rights to a fair and reliable capital trial with the qualified First Amendment rights of _potential lay witnesses_ , the group of persons the State seems to be most concerned about in the motion to amend.<sup>1</sup> Nor does the Publicity Order, in Mr. Robinson’s view, exceed the Court’s jurisdiction by imposing 

limitations on the public statements that potential lay witnesses may make about this matter. The motion should be denied.

It is also worth noting that while the Publicity Order imposes upon counsel for both parties a duty to take “reasonable care” (the term used in Rule 3.6) to prevent others associated with their respective teams not to make extrajudicial statements which if made by a lawyer would violate Rule 3.6, it does not purport to specifically order _non-attorneys_ to limit their speech such that they could be found in contempt for violating the order. The only persons whose action and inaction are subject to judicial supervision under the Publicity Order are the attorneys, and that is because the order merely makes specifically enforceable in this case that which is ethically required of all attorneys in Utah in every case, and of all prosecutors in Utah in every criminal case. _See In the Matter of Discipline of Leavitt_ , 2025 UT 46, ¶ 1, ___ P.3d ____ (Rule 3.6 “exists to prevent attorneys who are investigating or litigating a case from making extrajudicial statements that have a substantial likelihood of materially prejudicing the legal proceeding”).

**III. THE ORDER IS NEITHER VAGUE NOR OVERBROAD.** 

The State devotes much of its motion to amend to assertions that the order is vague, and therefore void, and that it is overbroad. It is neither. It appears that the State has latched onto the very recent decision of the Arkansas Supreme Court, _Spencer v. State_ , 712 S.W.3d 296 (Ark. 2025), which it cites in support of its overbreadth and vagueness arguments, but the gag order at issue in _Spencer_ and the order issued by this Court bear no resemblance to each other. _See id._ at 301-02. The “gag order” as the trial court referred to its own order in that case prohibited all public officials from making comments about the case, sealed the entire proceedings, and precluded the defendant’s “family” (a term the Supreme Court referred to as vague) from commenting as well. _Id._ at 303. 

If the State asserts that the Publicity Order is unconstitutionally vague or overbroad, then what it is really attacking is Rules 3.6 and 3.8 of the Utah Rules of Professional Conduct since, as pointed out above, the Publicity Order is primarily effectuating a standard of professional conduct in this case that is already imposed upon attorneys in all cases in Utah and upon all prosecutors in criminal cases in Utah.

In the motion to amend, the State asks a number of questions in its motion about how it should operationalize the Court’s use of the term “witness.” However, this is neither a vague nor overbroad term. It is clear that in a criminal prosecution, a witness is someone who has personal or specialized information that may be presented in court by them, either by way of live testimony or affidavit, or that may be relied upon by another person who presents that information in court. A witness is not, as the State suggests, only those who in the days just before trial are under subpoena. That criterion for the resolution of the question of who is and who is not a witness is both too narrow and would be resolved too late in the proceedings for the court’s order to have any meaningful impact on the goal of reducing the risk that extrajudicial statements may prejudice Mr. Robinson’s right to a fair trial. The State’s duty to inform its potential lay witnesses, and others, exists and must be fulfilled now, and not only in the days just before trial when subpoenas are served. 

The Tenth Circuit provides some guidance on how the State should fulfill its obligation under the Publicity Order. That court has ruled that the constitutional balance between the First Amendment rights of a non-attorney participant in a criminal proceeding and a defendant’s right to a fair trial are properly balanced where extrajudicial statements present a “reasonable likelihood” of prejudicing a fair trial. _United States v. Tijerina_ , 412 F.2d 661, 667 (10th Cir. 1969). This reasonable likelihood standard is actually more protective of a defendant’s right to a fair trial than is Utah Rule of Professional Conduct 3.6, which prohibits statements which have a “substantial likelihood of materially prejudicing” a proceeding.<sup>5</sup>

# **IV. THE STATE IS FAR MORE ABLE TO IDENTIFY WHO ITS POTENTIAL LAY WITNESSES ARE THAN ITS MOTION SUGGESTS** 

The motion to amend suggests that the State and its attorneys have no idea who their 

witnesses would be at any stage of this proceeding, placing them at risk of violating the order or imposing upon them an untenable burden. Given the State’s stated desire to convene a preliminary hearing as soon as possible, and its estimate of the time that a preliminary hearing will require, it is difficult to imagine that it does not have a reasonable belief as to who its witnesses may be at that hearing. 

Moreover, the State has already provided Mr. Robinson with a vast amount of discovery, much of which identifies potential lay witnesses who saw or heard something that may be admissible at trial. The bare assertion that over 3,000 individuals were present at the event where Mr. Kirk was shot is a gross exaggeration of the witness identification and selection project facing the State’s prosecution team. Rules of evidence that preclude the presentation of unnecessarily cumulative evidence or unfairly prejudicial evidence of limited probative value likely rule out 99 percent of those attendees, as the State well knows. _See_ Utah Rules of Evidence 401, 402, 403. Should there be a penalty phase of a trial in this case, the list of potential lay witnesses who may testify is also relatively easily constructed, even if it is longer and broader than pre-trial rulings and trial strategy decisions may ultimately dictate. 

Overall, the State’s burden (like the defense’s burden) under the Publicity Order is not 

unclear or insurmountable, particularly in light of the duty of the trial participants to preserve the integrity of the proceeding and uphold Mr. Robinson’s rights attendant to a fair trial and reliable penalty determination.

# **CONCLUSION** 

It is unclear if the State’s concern is that its attorneys and agents don’t have the _ability_ to control their potential lay witnesses and that the effort to seek death may somehow be prejudiced by other agendas, or if the State is concerned that its potential lay witnesses, some of whom may be among the highest profile government officials and public pundits in the country, may be frustrated by limitations on their right to both speak extrajudicially about the matter for their own personal benefit, and then participate in the trial as witnesses. 

The State’s assertion that “public statements by lay witnesses do not present the same concerns as statements by the parties or their team members” is simply false. _See_ Motion to Amend, at 4. The context of this case, namely, 1) the public notoriety of the victim, Mr. Kirk, and those close to him, 2) the politicization of his death and the prosecution of Mr. Robinson by persons who occupy a wide range of positions on the political spectrum, and 3) the “unprecedented” media interest in this trial, collectively demonstrate why public statements by potential lay witnesses present no less of a concern than do statements by counsel, their agents, and government officials associated with the investigation and prosecution. 

The motion to amend should be denied.
