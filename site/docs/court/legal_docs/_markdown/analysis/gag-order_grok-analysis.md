The gag order in the State v. Tyler Robinson case (Utah, 2025), stemming from the assassination of conservative activist Charlie Kirk at a public event, does indeed apply to the approximately 3,000 in-person witnesses and attendees present when the shooting occurred. The order is designed to prevent pretrial publicity that could taint the jury pool or compromise the investigation, explicitly extending to all potential witnesses— including event-goers who may have seen or recorded the incident—to restrict them from speaking to the media or publicly discussing case details. This broad scope was issued by Judge Tony Graf without formal requests from either the prosecution or defense, and it includes the risk of contempt charges for violations. 

This gag order is, by a wide margin, the largest in U.S. history for a criminal trial in terms of the number of people directly affected. No prior gag order in a U.S. criminal case has been documented as applying to anywhere near 3,000 individuals—most are limited to a small circle of core participants (e.g., the defendant, attorneys, law enforcement, and a handful of key witnesses), rarely exceeding 50 people. The unprecedented scale here stems from the public nature of the shooting at a large Turning Point USA event, where thousands were present, creating a massive pool of potential witnesses. Critics have argued it overly restricts free speech, while supporters claim it's necessary given the high-profile, politically charged nature of the case and the volume of potential evidence (e.g., cellphone videos and eyewitness accounts). In comparison, even in major mass shooting trials or celebrity cases with intense media scrutiny, gag orders have never swept in thousands of bystanders or attendees en masse.
