# **SUMMARY OF OPINIONS** 

1. News exposure in the United States is now frequently ambient and incidental: many individuals encounter news while using online platforms primarily for other purposes, such as 

3 

entertainment, social connection, or shopping. 

2. Algorithmic ranking and personalization increase the likelihood that residents of a 

particular geographic area will be exposed to online coverage and discussion of locally salient events more often than people outside that area. Local relevance and local network activity are commonly used signals in content selection and ranking. 

3. As a result, local residents often do not need to seek out local news. Instead, they can be repeatedly exposed through feeds, recommended modules, and notifications, as well as through social sharing within local networks. This dynamic also makes sustained avoidance during trial more difficult than in legacy media environments. 

4. Online environments frequently include large volumes of user-generated commentary that may be less moderated than print or broadcast channels. Empirical research shows that exposure to uncivil or hostile online commentary can shift perceptions and intensify reactions to the underlying information. 

5. A substantial body of psychology-and-law research, including meta-analyses, finds 

that negative pretrial publicity can bias juror judgments, increase the likelihood of guilty verdicts in experimental settings, and contribute to memory and source-monitoring errors, including misattributing pretrial information as trial evidence. 

# **BACKGROUND AND EXPLANATION** 

6. The modern online news environment differs from legacy print and broadcast media in two practical respects: (1) news is persistent, searchable, and easily redistributed, and (2) news is routinely delivered through algorithmically curated feeds and recommendations rather than solely through intentional consumption choices. 

7. In legacy media, many exposures required purposeful action: purchasing or reading a 

4 

newspaper, turning on a scheduled broadcast, or visiting a news website. In contrast, major social and video platforms now interweave news with entertainment and social content, so that exposure can occur during ordinary, non-news online activity. 

8. Peer-reviewed research on incidental news exposure documents that encountering 

news on social platforms without deliberately seeking it is common and has become a 

meaningful pathway by which people learn about current events. 

9. This matters in a high-profile case because potential jurors may have been exposed not 

only to formal news reporting, but also to headlines, clips, reposts, and discussions that appear in feeds and recommendations. Such exposure may be repeated and may occur without the individual intending to consume case-related information. 

# **ALGORITHMIC CURATION AND GEOGRAPHICALLY LOCALIZED EXPOSURE** 

10. Most major platforms use ranking and recommendation systems, known as 

algorithms, to choose which items to show a user and in what order. These systems evaluate many candidate items and prioritize those predicted to be most engaging or relevant to that user, based on signals such as prior engagement, network behavior, popularity, and contextual factors. 

11. The objectives of these ranking and recommendation systems are closely tied to the 

business models of the platforms that deploy them. Many major internet and social media platforms are substantially supported by advertising revenue, and their financial incentives increase when they can keep users’ attention for longer periods and deliver more effective targeted advertising. As a result, these systems are engineered to predict and maximize 

measurable engagement and to improve targeting performance, often by inferring user attributes, interests, and contexts (including location-related signals) that can be used to select and prioritize content most likely to capture attention. Peer-reviewed research in marketing and economics 

5 

documents that targeting and personalization can materially affect advertising effectiveness and thus platform profitability, and that the use of personal data and privacy tradeoffs are integral to this advertising-supported ecosystem. 

12. Personalization can be empirically measured. Research has demonstrated that outputs such as search results and news selections can vary across users and contexts, including variation attributable to historical account usage and other contextual signals that can correlate with a user’s environment. 

13. In the context of a salient local event, residents are more likely to be connected to local sources and local networks that generate and circulate case-related content, and their platforms are more likely to infer local relevance. These factors increase the probability of repeated exposure among local residents relative to non-residents. 

14. Accordingly, a resident may encounter case-related publicity while using the internet to view unrelated content or to communicate with others. The mechanisms are not limited to deliberate searching; they include recommendation modules, trending panels, and reposts or commentary surfaced because of local engagement 

# **ONLINE PUBLICITY CAN BE MORE INFLAMMATORY AND HARDER TO AVOID** 

15. Online platforms routinely provide user comment features and social feedback mechanisms (likes, shares, replies). The result is that case-related reporting is often accompanied 

by user-generated commentary that may include speculation, misinformation, and expressions of strong bias. 

16. Experimental research indicates that hostile or uncivil comments can affect readers’ interpretations and perceptions. In practical terms, the same news item may be encountered alongside inflammatory commentary that can heighten emotional response and shape 

6 

impressions beyond the factual content of the reporting. 

17. Avoidance is also more difficult online than in print or broadcast settings because exposure can be incidental and interwoven with everyday uses of platforms. Even a user who does not seek case coverage may still encounter it through algorithmic resurfacing and social sharing, particularly when a topic is locally prominent. 

# **PRETRIAL PUBLICITY EFFECTS ON JUROR DECISION-MAKING** 

18. A large body of empirical research in psychology and law finds that exposure to 

pretrial publicity can bias juror decision-making. Meta-analytic research indicates that negative pretrial publicity increases the likelihood of guilty judgments in experimental studies. 

19. Research also identifies cognitive mechanisms relevant to juror impartiality, 

including memory and source-monitoring problems in which individuals later confuse whether information came from media exposure or from trial evidence. Such effects can persist even when individuals intend to be fair and to follow instructions. 

20. The contemporary online environment exacerbates these concerns because it 

increases volume, repetition, and emotional intensity of exposure, and because social endorsement cues(likes, shares, comments) elevate perceived consensus or certainty around narratives. 

# **IMPLICATIONS FOR THE PRESENT MOTION** 

21. In considering the present motion, one of the factors the Court is directed to consider 

under Utah Rule of Judicial Administration 4-401.01 is “whether there is a reasonable likelihood that electronic media coverage will prejudice the right of the parties to a fair proceeding.” Dr. Edelman’s opinion is that contemporary online systems, which find an important visual source in 

7 

electronic media coverage of court proceedings, affect the likelihood and pathways of exposure to local-case publicity and the feasibility of avoidance. 

22. From a technical and behavioral perspective, a locally salient high-profile case is 

more likely to produce widespread and repeated exposure among local residents because of algorithmic curation and local social sharing, as described above. 

23. These dynamics bear on two practical considerations relevant to analysis of the 

motion: (1) the extent to which a local jury pool may have been exposed to case-related electronic coverage and commentary, and (2) the practical ability of jurors to avoid additional exposure during trial given the structure of online platforms. Algorithmic targeting is not only driven by geographic location, but by a wide array of behavioral signals including prior attention and engagement (like reading articles, watching video, or writing a comment). Online platforms use this data to predict what will keep a given user viewing, clicking, and sharing. In a case that is locally salient over an extended period, residents in the originating region are more likely, on average, to have accumulated a multi-year history of direct and indirect engagement signals (e.g., clicks, watch time, searches, shares, or repeated exposure through friends and local pages) that can cause platforms’ systems to treat the topic as personally relevant and therefore worthy of repeated placement. 

# **CONCLUSION** 

24. For the reasons stated, it is Dr. Edelman’s opinion that the modern internet and social media ecosystem—especially algorithmic curation and personalization—has fundamentally altered how news is consumed and makes local, high-profile publicity substantially harder to avoid for residents of the locality where the events giving rise to the case occurred and the case is being tried.
