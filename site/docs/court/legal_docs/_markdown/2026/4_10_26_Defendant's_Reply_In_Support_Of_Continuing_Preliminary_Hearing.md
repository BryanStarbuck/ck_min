# **INTRODUCTION**

As explained below, the State’s assertion that these reports are admissible before the underlying data upon which they rely has been produced to and meaningfully evaluated by the defense makes a mockery of article I, section 12 of the Utah Constitution. Admission of these reports pursuant to Utah Rule of Evidence 1102(b)(4) under the current conditions clearly violates the Eighth Amendment’s requirement of “heightened reliability” in all aspects of a capital proceeding, as well as Mr. Robison’s right to due process.

# **IV. The State’s Interpretation of Its Constitutional and Rule-Based Discovery Obligations Is Incorrect; the State Is Responsible for Seeking Out and Providing What Is Known and Requested.** 

The State’s argument regarding its obligations under Utah Rule of Criminal Procedure 

16, the governing rule anticipated by article I, section 12 of the Utah Constitution, is an effort to relieve it of its burden to produce evidence to which Mr. Robinson is constitutionally entitled, is 

known to the prosecution, and that has been “presently and reasonably available” to the prosecution either before charges were filed against Mr. Robinson in September of 2025, or very shortly thereafter. The State’s efforts to relieve itself of its constitutional obligation to provide the requested discovery create an untenable loophole in Rule 16 and Utah Constitution, article I, section 12 that results in no constitutional protections at all. _See Opp_ . at 10–12. 

The State makes much of the phrase in Rule 16(a)(2), “to the prosecutor” in the following: 

In every case, all material or information listed under paragraph (a)(1) that is presently and reasonably available to the prosecutor must be disclosed before the preliminary examination, if applicable, or before the defendant enters a plea of guilty or no contest or goes to trial, unless otherwise waived by the defendant. 

Page **12** of **17** 

The prosecution interprets this to mean that the only evidence it need produce to the defense prior to the preliminary hearing is that which its lawyers physically have in their possession. However, this argument ignores the plain language incorporating the (a)(1) _mandatory_ disclosures and undermines the State’s constitutional obligation to go beyond its own files to those of the entire prosecution team as part of its _ongoing_ discovery obligations. _See, e.g., Kyles v. Whitley_ , 514 U.S. 419, 437 (1995) (“the individual prosecutor has a duty to learn of any favorable evidence known to the others acting on the government’s behalf in the case, including the police.”). As a policy matter, such an interpretation would encourage or at least excuse bad faith decisions by the State to do the opposite—to intentionally avoid seeking information from its law enforcement partners prior to the preliminary hearing so it does not have to disclose it. Mr. Robinson is not suggesting here that there has been bad faith by the State’s attorneys with respect to discovery prosecution, but that is not the standard. 

Mr. Robinson has never argued, as the State suggests, that discovery must be complete 

before a preliminary hearing. _Cf_ . _Opp_ . at 7. Mr. Robinson does argue that the State must disclose evidence the defense _has requested_ since its initial discovery request in October of 2025, that is _known to be in possession of the police-prosecution team_ , and to which the defense reasonably believes is _necessary to render effective assistance of counsel_ and to ensure Mr. Robinson a constitutionally rigorous and reliable preliminary hearing. _See State v. Thomas_ , 1999 UT 2, ¶ 24, 974 P.2d 269 (“In the past, we have stressed the importance that the prosecution has a duty to provide discovery materials to the defense on request . . . Furthermore, rule 3.8(d) of the Rules of Professional Conduct dictates that a prosecutor shall ‘[m]ake timely disclosure to the defense of all evidence or information known to the prosecutor that tends to negate the guilt of the accused or mitigates the offense.’” (additional citation omitted)). Whether these disclosures are what the

State would likewise provide prior to calling an expert at trial is irrelevant. The issue presented is what the defense requires to render effective assistance of counsel at a constitutionally reliable preliminary hearing in a capital case and again, the defense has made it abundantly clear that it cannot meet these constitutional obligations, particularly under the strictures of the Eighth Amendment, without the discovery repeatedly requested. 

Further, Mr. Robinson’s plain language interpretation of Rule 16 and Utah Constitution, article I, section 12, that requires sufficient discovery prior to reliance on “reliable hearsay” does not cause chaos, as the State suggests. This interpretation doesn’t mandate the completion of all expert investigation and discovery before a preliminary hearing.
