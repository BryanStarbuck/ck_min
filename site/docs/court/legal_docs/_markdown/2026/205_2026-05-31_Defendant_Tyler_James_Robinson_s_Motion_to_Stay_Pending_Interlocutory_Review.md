Defendant, Tyler James Robinson (“Mr. Robinson”), by and through undersigned counsel, hereby files this motion to stay further proceedings in this case, including the preliminary hearing presently scheduled for the week of July 6, 2026, pending review of his _Petition for Permission to Appeal from an Interlocutory Order_ , filed May 29, 2026. Mr. Robinson is seeking appellate review of this Court’s _Ruling and Order Denying Motion to Exclude Still Photographs, TV Cameras and Microphones from the Courtroom (Dkt. 305) and Standing Order on Electronic Media Coverage Requests_ (“ _Camera Ruling_ ”). _See_ Dkt. 535. This motion is brought pursuant to Utah Rule of Appellate Procedure 8 and grounded in the Fifth, Sixth, Eighth and Fourteenth Amendments to the United States Constitution and Article I, sections 7, 9, and 12 of the Utah Constitution. 

## **FACTUAL GROUNDS** 

Due to the overwhelming negative publicity generated by the electronic media coverage (“EMC”) of Mr. Robinson’s case, he sought an order from this Court prohibiting further EMC of the hearings in this capital case. _See_ Dkt. 305; _see also_ Dkt 399 ( _Reply in Support of Motion_ ). The State of Utah and the News Media objected to Mr. Robinson’s request. _See_ Dkts. 354-64, 350, 440. An evidentiary hearing was held on April 17, 2026. _See_ Dkt. 512 ( _Transcript_ **_of:_** _Motion Hearing (4/17/26)_ ). The Court limited each party to two hours “to present argument, examine witnesses, cross-examination, and redirect and respond to inquiries from the Court as well as reserve time for rebuttal.” _Id_ . at 8. 

In the time provided, Mr. Robinson introduced into evidence: truncated testimony and written reports and declarations by Dr. Ruva and trial consultant Dr. Brian Edelman; two lengthy PowerPoint presentations, one of which contained multiple examples of prejudicial electronic media coverage and of an instance where the State had violated the court’s publicity order; all of 

Page **2** of **8** 

the studies cited in Dr. Ruva’s declaration, supplemental report, and PowerPoint; over 6000 pages of transcripts of the electronic media coverage in this case; all of the electronic media clips referenced in the motion; the declaration of Mr. Robinson’s lead counsel setting forth threats she had received as a result of the publicity; and the declarations of various witnesses who objected to having their images or testimony broadcast, or experienced adverse effects from pretrial publicity. 

On May 8, 2026, the Court issued its _Camera Ruling_ denying Mr. Robinson’s requested relief. Dkt. 535. The denial relied on the Court’s interpretation of Utah Rule of Judicial Administration 4-401.01 but failed to address Mr. Robinson’s constitutional arguments. _Id_ . The Court found only that a blanket ruling prohibiting EMC was not permissible under Rule 4- 401.01, leaving open the possibility of either or both parties challenging EMC prior to every hearing. However, the Court outlined a procedure for doing so that deprives the parties of any meaningful opportunity to object, to be heard, and to seek review of an adverse order to a higher court before the damage is done. 

Mr. Robinson filed a petition seeking interlocutory review of the Court’s _Camera Ruling_ on May 29, 2026. _See_ Exhibit A ( _Petition_ ). In his _Petition_ , Mr. Robinson asks the Utah Supreme Court to grant interlocutory review for three reasons: (1) review is necessary to protect Mr. Robinson’s fundamental federal and state constitutional rights to a fair and reliable trial by an impartial jury at both phases of his capital case; (2) it was error to deny Mr. Robinson’s requested relief without reaching the constitutional issues; (3) this Court’s imposition of severe procedural restrictions violates Mr. Robinson’s state and federal constitutional rights to procedural due process.

With a lengthy preliminary hearing set to begin in five weeks and four justices of the Utah Supreme Court in transition, it is unlikely that Mr. Robinson’s _Petition_ will be resolved prior to the July 6-10, 2026 preliminary hearing. This leaves Mr. Robinson in the untenable position of proceeding with a preliminary hearing with EMC attendant<sup>1</sup> in violation of his constitutional rights while the _Petition_ seeking to prevent this harm remains pending. It is for this reason that Mr. Robinson seeks to stay proceedings pending the outcome of his _Petition_ . 

## **POINTS AND AUTHORITIES** 

“It lies within the inherent powers of the courts to grant a stay of proceedings. It is a discretionary power, and the grounds therefor necessarily vary according to the requirements of each individual case.” _Lewis v. Moultree_ , 627 P.2d 94, 96 (Utah 1981). Here, the Court should exercise its discretion in granting a stay in order to prevent the prejudice to Mr. Robinson and the violation of his constitutional rights that will inevitably result from the continued EMC of these capital proceedings. 

In granting interlocutory review of a motion to change venue, the Utah Supreme Court in _State v. James_ noted the important purpose of interlocutory appeal as affording “the opportunity to review the denial before the error committed would be prejudicial to the defendant.” _State v. James_ , 767 P.2d 549, 555 (Utah 1989). And, in circumstances where prejudice may be prevented, like here, the Utah Supreme Court aligns with the United States Supreme Court “that ‘reversals are but palliatives’ and that ‘the cure lies in those remedial measures that will prevent the prejudice at its inception.’” _Id_ . at 555-56 (quoting _Sheppard v. Maxwell_ , 384 U.S. 333, 363 (1966)). The same reasoning supporting the grant of interlocutory appeal supports staying these

The United States Supreme Court granted a stay request in the case _Hollingsworth v._ 

_Perry_ , 558 U.S. 183 (2010) (per curiam). In _Hollingsworth_ , the Court determined that an order permitting the broadcast of a federal trial should be stayed because the district court failed to follow the appropriate procedure to alter the rule prohibiting such broadcasting. _Id_ . at 184-85. The Court emphasized that the harm from broadcasting could not be undone: 

Applicants also have shown that irreparable harm will likely result from the denial of the stay. Without a stay, the District Court will broadcast the trial. It would be difficult—if not impossible—to reverse the harm from those broadcasts… This Court has recognized that witness testimony may be chilled if broadcast. _See Estes v. Texas_ , [381 U.S. 532, 547 (1965); _id_ . at 591 (Harlan, J., concurring)]. Some of applicants’ witnesses have already said that they will not testify if the trial is broadcast, and they have substantiated their concerns by citing incidents of past harassment…. These concerns are not diminished by the fact that some of applicants’ witnesses are compensated expert witnesses. There are qualitative differences between making public appearances regarding an issue and having one’s testimony broadcast throughout the country. _Applicants may not be able to obtain adequate relief through an appeal._ The trial will have already been broadcast. It is difficult to demonstrate or analyze whether a witness would have testified differently if his or her testimony had not been broadcast. And witnesses subject to harassment as a result of broadcast of their testimony might be less likely to cooperate in any future proceedings. 

_Id_ . at 195 (emphasis added). 

As in _Hollingsworth_ , if proceedings continue with EMC coverage through the pendency of his _Petition_ , the prejudice to Mr. Robinson evidenced in his lengthy motion, reply, and 

witness materials, will continue and will be compounded. As expert witness Dr. Christine Ruva testified, this case is already “situated within a highly prejudicial pretrial environment that presents a substantial risk to juror impartiality.” Dkt. 512, at 196. Now, this Court is poised to add to the prejudicial publicity flowing from the courtroom with the impending preliminary hearing where the State intends to proceed with a presentation almost exclusively comprised of

# **<u>INTRODUCTION</u>** 

Petitioner, Tyler James Robinson (“Mr. Robinson”) seeks permission to appeal the 

interlocutory order issued by the Honorable Tony F. Graf, Jr., of the Fourth Judicial 

District Court denying Mr. Robinson’s _Motion to Exclude Still Photographers, TV Cameras and Microphones from the Courtroom_ . _See_ Addendum A ( _Ruling and Order on Defendant’s Motion to Exclude Still Photographers, TV Cameras and Microphones from the Courtroom_ (Docket 305)); Add. B ( _Motion to Exclude_ ). 

# **<u>JURISDICTION</u>** 

Mr. Robinson is charged with a capital felony offense, aggravated homicide. Add. 

H ( _Information_ ). This Court has original appellate jurisdiction over “interlocutory appeals 

from any court of record involving a charge of a first degree or capital felony.” Utah 

Code § 78A-3-102(3)(h). “[A]n appeal of an interlocutory order of a court of record 

involving a charge of a capital felony” is excluded from this Court’s transfer authority. Utah Code § 78A-3-102(4)(a). 

# **<u>STATEMENT OF THE CASE</u>** 

Mr. Robinson is charged, _inter alia_ , by _Information_ with the aggravated murder of 

Charlie Kirk on September 10, 2025, in violation of Utah Code § 76-5-202. Add. H.; _see_ Utah Code § 76-5-202. Concurrent with the filing of the _Information_ , the State filed 

notice of its intention to seek the death penalty against Mr. Robinson. Add. I. 

Early in the case, it became apparent to both the parties and the district court that 

“[t]he media interest in Mr. Robinson’s trial is arguably unprecedented in the State of 

Utah.” _See_ Add. L (Dkt. 137). The district court acknowledged that images of Mr. 

1 

Robinson from the courtroom “will likely be shared and disseminated in various public platforms” and “[t]he danger of prejudice is compounded by technological advancements that allow the images to be manipulated in ways that do not accurately reflect the truth of the court proceedings.” _Id_ . at 5. The case is so unique that all of Mr. Robinson’s hearings are held as special settings. _Id_ . 

Despite multiple decorum orders intended to curb prejudicial media practices in the courtroom, the news media violated the orders on multiple occasions. _See_ Add. J; _see, e.g._ , Add. B, at 31-35. As a result, in a lengthy motion that featured multiple examples of prejudicial electronic media coverage, showing both the violations of the decorum orders and how the media is using the courtroom recordings and livestream to prejudice Mr. Robinson’s rights, Mr. Robinson sought to exclude still photography, cameras, and microphones from all of his hearings to ensure that he receives his constitutional guarantees of a fair trial and reliable penalty proceeding. _See generally_ , Add. B. The motion was supported by the declaration of cognitive psychologist Dr. Christine Ruva, one of the country’s leading researchers on the effects of prejudicial publicity, who 

reviewed the existing empirical research on multiple issues relevant to the motion, and by the declarations of two critical witnesses who indicated they had been harassed by the media and did not consent to have their testimony or images broadcast. _Id_ . at Exhibit 2; _also_ Add. K ( _Exhibits from 4-17-2026 Hearing_ ).

This opportunity to provide needed appellate guidance arises in a capital case, where the United States Supreme Court has held that “[w]hen a defendant’s life is at stake, the Court has been particularly sensitive to insure that every safeguard is observed.” _Gregg v. - **A. This Court Should Grant Interlocutory Review Because It Is Necessary to Protect Mr. Robinson’s Fundamental State and Federal Constitutional Rights to a Fair and Reliable Trial by an Impartial Jury at Both Phases of His Capital Case.** 

In _State v. James_ , where the denial of a motion to change venue was brought to the 

Court as an interlocutory appeal, this Court ruled that the interlocutory appeal afforded 

…the opportunity to review the denial before any error committed would be prejudicial to defendant. We are in accord with a statement made by the Supreme Court of the United States in _Sheppard v. Maxwell_ , [384 U.S. 333, 363 (1966)], that ‘reversals are but palliatives’ and that ‘the cure lies in those remedial measures that will prevent the prejudice at its inception.’ 

767 P.2d at 555-66; _see also State v. Stubbs_ , 2005 UT 65, ¶¶ 9-10, 123 P.3d 407 (noting 

that in _James_ , this Court “welcomed the interlocutory review of a venue challenge 

because it presented us with ‘the opportunity to review the denial before any error committed would be prejudicial to [the] defendant.’”). 

The Court’s reliance on _Sheppard_ is significant because in the portion of that 

opinion quoted by this Court in _James_ , 767 P.2d at 556, the Supreme Court emphasized that both trial and appellate courts have a duty to take prompt remedial steps to protect a defendant’s fundamental right to a fair trial by an impartial jury: 

Given the pervasiveness of modern communications and the difficulty of effacing prejudicial publicity from the minds of the jurors, the trial courts must take strong measures to ensure that the balance is never weighed against the accused. _And appellate tribunals have the duty to make an independent evaluation of the circumstances_ … If publicity during the proceedings threatens the fairness of the trial, a new trial should be ordered. 

11 

But we must remember that reversals are but palliatives; the cure lies in those remedial measures that will prevent the prejudice at its inception. The courts must take such steps by rule and regulation that will protect their processes from prejudicial outside interferences. 

_Sheppard_ , 384 U.S. at 362-63 (emphasis added). _Accord Nebraska Press Ass’n v. Stuart_ , 

427 U.S. 539, 555 (1976) (“The costs of failure to afford a fair trial are high… The 

‘strong measures’ outlined in _Sheppard v. Maxwell_ are means by which a trial judge can try to avoid exacting these costs from society or from the accused.”). 

The reasoning of _James_ in favor of interlocutory review of orders denying motions for change of venue has been extended to review of orders allowing cameras in the courtroom. Most notably, in _Hollingsworth v. Perry_ , the Court took the unusual step of granting a stay of a district court order allowing the televising of the civil trial pending the timely filing and disposition of a petition for a writ of certiorari or the filing and disposition of a petition for a writ of mandamus. 558 U.S. at 184. Addressing the issue of irreparable harm, the Court concluded: 

Applicants also have shown that irreparable harm will likely result from the denial of the stay. Without a stay, the District Court will broadcast the trial. It would be difficult—if not impossible—to reverse the harm from those broadcasts… This Court has recognized that witness testimony may be chilled if broadcast. _See Estes v. Texas_ , [381 U.S. 532, 547 (1965); _id_ . at 591 (Harlan, J., concurring)]. Some of applicants’ witnesses have already said that they will not testify if the trial is broadcast, and they have substantiated their concerns by citing incidents of past harassment…. These concerns are not diminished by the fact that some of applicants’ witnesses are compensated expert witnesses. There are qualitative differences between making public appearances regarding an issue and having one’s testimony broadcast throughout the country. Applicants may not be able to obtain adequate relief through an appeal. The trial will have already been broadcast. It is difficult to demonstrate or analyze whether a witness would have testified differently if his or her testimony had not been broadcast. 

12 

And witnesses subject to harassment as a result of broadcast of their testimony might be less likely to cooperate in any future proceedings. 

_Id_ . at 195. 

_Hollingsworth_ cites with approval _In re Sony BMG Music Ent._ , 564 F.3d 1, 2 (1st Cir. 2009). _Id_ . at 194, 198. In that case, the First Circuit addressed a question of first impression: “does a federal district judge have the authority to permit gavel-to-gavel webcasting of a hearing in a civil case?” 564 F.3d at 2. Before answering “no” to that question, the court first considered the issue of whether the case was an appropriate one

Journal Co._ , 293 F.3d 1, 9 (1st Cir. 2002)). The power of a district court to order narrowcasting of a hearing in a civil case is such an issue: it is systemically important and rife with implications for the public interest. In the absence of definitive guidance from this court, the issue is increasingly likely to recur within the district courts of this circuit. These circumstances counsel in favor of an exercise of our advisory mandamus jurisdiction. _See, e.g._ , _In re Sterling–Suarez_ , 306 F.3d 1170, 1172 (1st Cir. 2002).

But even while subscribing to that tenet, we believe that this is an appropriate case in which to consider granting the writ. Both the rapidity of technological change and the widespread interest that this proceeding has attracted argue persuasively for a prompt and authoritative resolution of the systemically important issue that lies at the epicenter of this dispute. 

_Id_ . at 4. _See also Heckstall v. McGrath_ , 15 A.D.3d 824, 826 (N.Y. App. Div. 2005) (“A
