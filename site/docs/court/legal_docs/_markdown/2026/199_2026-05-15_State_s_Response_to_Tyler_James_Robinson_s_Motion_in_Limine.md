The State of Utah, by and through its counsel, Chad E. Grunander, Deputy Utah County Attorney, hereby responds to Defendant’s Motion in Limine to Close Preliminary Hearing Testimony and Seal Exhibits (“Motion”). 

# **<u>ARGUMENT</u>** 

This Court should deny Defendant’s Motion in part and grant it in part. Specifically, the request to close even portions of the hearing should be denied because: (1) the public and the media possess constitutional rights to access preliminary hearings, subject to limitations not applicable here; and (2) Defendant has failed to comply with the standards and procedures for restricting media coverage of the hearing under UCJA Rule 4-401.01 and this Court’s Order. However, the States agrees that consistent with Utah case law, the Court should not permit the 

public or the press to physically inspect or copy the preliminary hearing exhibits, as such access would not serve the administration of justice. 

- **I. Defendant Has failed to Demonstrate that the Public’s and Media’s Access to the Preliminary Hearing Poses a Realistic Likelihood of Prejudice to His Right to a Fair Trial.** 

In _Kearns-Tribune Corp. v. Lewis_ , 685 P.2d 515 (Utah 1984), the Utah Supreme Court 

held that “the public’s (including the media’s) First Amendment right of access applies to preliminary hearings in criminal cases.” _Id._ at 520.<sup>1</sup> The Court also held that both the public and the press have a constitutional right to access preliminary hearings under the Utah Constitution, subject to certain exceptions. _Id._ at 521-22. A trial court can deny access to a preliminary hearing _if_ it finds that the publicity would pose a “realistic likelihood of prejudice to a fair trial ….” _Id._ at

522 (citing _State v. Williams_ , 93 N.J. 63, 459 A.2d 641 (1983)). The burden of proof to close a preliminary hearing is on the party moving for closure. The Court outlined the following two

- (1) the evidence relevant to the nature and extent of the adverse publicity [that may be] generated by the open pretrial proceedings, including any inferences as to its potential for prejudice against a fair trial by an impartial jury, and

In _State v. Archuleta_ , 857 P.2d 234 (Utah 1993), the Utah Supreme Court reaffirmed the

Under the controlling legal standard, the burden rests squarely on Defendant to show that closure is the only viable way to protect his right to a fair trial.<sup>3</sup> But he has offered only vague assertions. Because Defendant has failed to make the necessary showing on either analytical factor, his Motion to close even portions of the preliminary hearing should be denied.

# **III. Defendant Has Not Overcome the Legal Presumption that Electronic Media Coverage is Permitted at Preliminary Hearings.** 

Utah Code of Judicial Administration (UCJA) Rule 4-401.01 outlines uniform standards and procedures for electronic media coverage of court proceedings. Subsection (2)(A) creates a presumption that “electronic media coverage by a news reporter shall be permitted in public proceedings where the predominant purpose of the . . . coverage request is journalism or dissemination of news to the public.” To overcome this presumption, trial courts must consider some or all of the factors outlined in subsection (2)(B). Moreover, this Court’s Order requires any party seeking to restrict electronic media coverage of a proceeding to file a motion under Rule 4-401.01. _See_ Dkt. 535 at 8. 

The preliminary hearing in this matter is a public proceeding. Consequently, the media has a right to be present and to report on the hearing. As the Utah Supreme Court stated in 

7 

_Archuletta_ , “[t]he Press is certainly entitled to attend preliminary hearings in appropriate circumstances and to report all that occurs there.” 857 P.2d at 242. Closure of even portions of the hearing would restrict the media’s access, thereby implicating UCJA Rule 4-401.01. Here, Defendant not only failed to address the factors outlined in Rule 4-401.01, but he also failed to even reference the rule in his Motion. Defendant has therefore failed to comply with this Court’s Order requiring him to file a motion under the rule to restrict media access. Accordingly, 

Defendant has not met his burden and his Motion to close the hearing or any portions of it should be denied. 

- **IV. The Court Should Restrict the Public and the Media from Physically Inspecting or Copying the Preliminary Hearing Exhibits but Allow the Media to Report on What Occurs at the Hearing.** 

In _Archuleta_ , the press intervened in a murder prosecution to access pretrial documents and preliminary hearing exhibits. _See_ 857 P.2d at 234. The media asserted a right to “inspect and copy” all of the exhibits. _Id._ at 241. Some of the exhibits included the autopsy report, the medical examiner’s photos of the victim’s body, tissue, bone fragments, instruments used to torture and kill the victim, bloody clothing, and fingerprints from the crime scene. _Id._ The Court described the facts of the case and the evidence in question as “horrific,” a “torture-slaying,” “sensitive and inflammatory evidence or information,” “inadmissible and possibly inaccurate information,” and “speculative [in] nature.” _Id._ at 236, 239-40. While the Court reaffirmed the press’s right to attend preliminary hearings and “report all that occurs there,” it clarified that the First Amendment does not grant a presumptive right to physically inspect or copy exhibits from those hearings: 

Permitting the public [and the media] to parade into a courtroom to inspect . . . items of evidence that will conceivably be used at a subsequent trial would severely disrupt the efficient performance of justice and create obvious problems 

8 

concerning the court’s and the State’s mandate to adequately preserve the integrity of the evidence. 

_Id._ at 241-42. 

The Court also held that the public and the media are not entitled under the Utah 

Constitution to inspect preliminary hearing exhibits. The Court explained that “allowing blanket inspection of exhibits that must be preserved for a trial later if the defendant is bound over to district court _will hinder, not further, the interests of the criminal process_ .” _Id._ at 242 (emphasis added). 

While the Court held that the public and the media possess a common law right to inspect and copy preliminary hearing exhibits, it recognized that this right is not absolute and is subject to the “sound discretion of the trial court to be exercised in light of the relevant facts and circumstances of the particular case.” _Id._ at 242. The Court further concluded that providing the requested access to the exhibits “would not serve the administration of justice and … could very well jeopardize defendants’ right to a fair trial.” _Id._ 

Here, none of the State’s proposed evidence is speculative, inflammatory, or inaccurate, although the videos of the victim being shot are sensitive. And again, there is nothing to suggest that the substance of the evidence is inadmissible. Moreover, the evidence does not involve torture, horrific claims, or other information that would implicate rule 403 of the Utah Rules of Evidence.<sup>6</sup> However, because some of the exhibits are likely to be used at a subsequent trial, the public and the media should be restricted from physically examining or copying them. # **CONCLUSION** 

For these reasons, this Court should deny Defendant’s Motion to close even portions of the 

preliminary hearing but grant the Motion in part to restrict the public and the media from physically examining or copying the exhibits.
