To be more precise, as of April 1, 2026, counsel for the State had

# **ARGUMENT** 

> 1 The State is currently in the process of obtaining a small number 1102 statements from witnesses whose involvement was described in police reports. 

2 

Defendant argues that the preliminary hearing should be continued until expert discovery is complete and he is prepared to address it through cross-examination and his own expert witnesses. In essence, Defendant asks the Court to attach trial rights to the preliminary hearing. Defendant puts the cart before the horse: expert discovery obligations attach to the _final_ stages of pre-trial proceedings, not the initial ones. Contrary to Defendant’s contention for expansive preliminary hearing rights, the Constitution limits the preliminary hearing to only one purpose: the determination of probable case. The extent to which discovery and due process rights apply at the preliminary hearing must be viewed in light of this very narrow purpose. 

Accordingly, the preliminary hearing should go forward as scheduled. The State is ready to proceed with evidence establishing probable cause—i.e., evidence “support[ing] a reasonable belief that [the] offense[s] ha[ve] been committed and that the defendant committed” them. _State v. Lopez_ , 2020 UT 61, ¶ 50, 474 P.3d 949. Thereafter, the Court should not continue the hearing unless the delay is necessary to ensure an accurate determination of probable cause. This is even more important in this case because an unnecessary delay would violate the victim’s right, asserted by his duly designated representative, to a speedy trial. In this case, Defendant has not shown that a continuance will have any effect on the probable cause determination, and he has made no effort to do so. Instead, he has made a procedural argument that expansive due process rights attach to the very narrow hearing. But the law is clear—complete discovery is not required before the preliminary hearing. Further, the preliminary hearing is not the time to challenge expert testimony. 

3 

**1. The Court should adopt the** **_State v. Lopez_ analysis and deny Defendant’s motion because he has not shown that a continuance is necessary to ensure an accurate probable cause determination.** 

# **1.1.** **_State v. Lopez_ applies here.** 

In _State v. Lopez_ , the Utah Supreme Court held that a defendant does not have a right to subpoena a victim for a preliminary hearing – and that the trial court must quash such a subpoena – unless the defendant “demonstrates that the subpoena is necessary to present specific evidence that is reasonably likely to defeat the showing of probable cause.” 2020 UT 61, at ¶ 4. In reaching its decision, the court recognized: (1) the limited purpose of the preliminary hearing; (2) the admissibility of hearsay; (3) the low burden of proof, which does not allow the magistrate to weigh contradictory or impeachment evidence; and (4) the victim’s rights. _Id._ at ¶¶ 43-49. In summary, the court held, 

A defendant has the general authority to “call witnesses” at a preliminary hearing, but a subpoena compelling alleged victims to testify is per se “unreasonable” when it seeks testimony that is immaterial to the probable-cause determination, would obviate the legal sufficiency of hearsay evidence, and would unnecessarily intrude on the rights of victims. 

_Id._ at ¶ 53. 

The Court then explained the process for considering a motion to quash a subpoena of a victim at the preliminary hearing. First, the court should hold the hearing. _Id._ at ¶ 54. After making a prima facie probable cause determination, the judge should consider the motion to quash the subpoena. _Id._ To survive the motion to quash the subpoena, the defense must show “that additional live testimony from the victim is necessary to present evidence on a specific point material to the 

probable cause determination, and that is reasonably likely to defeat the State’s prima facie showing of probable cause.” _Id._ 

4 

_Lopez_ is directly analogous here because it dealt with the extent of due process required or 

even _allowed_ at the preliminary hearing in light of victims’ rights. The point of _Lopez,_ as it pertains to this case, is that the purpose of the preliminary hearing does not go beyond the determination of probable cause. Where a defense motion affects victims’ rights, the Court should strictly limit the hearing to its constitutional purpose. 

In sum, this Court should follow the framework established in _Lopez_ . The Court should (1) hold the preliminary hearing, _Id_ . at ¶ 54; (2) determine whether the State has satisfied the probable cause standard; and (3) if so, address Defendant’s motion to continue the preliminary hearing. _See id_ . at ¶ 54. 

# **1.2. Defendant has not shown that continuance is necessary.** 

Defendant has not indicated how additional discovery will defeat probable cause, which is a particularly difficult task because (1) rule 1102(b)(4) of the Utah Rules of Evidence deems “scientific, laboratory, or forensic reports and records” to be reliable hearsay admissible without foundation or the testimony of their author, and (2) because the Court does not weigh contrary testimony and must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the State. _State v. Virgin_ , 137 P.3d 787, 793, 2006 UT 29, ¶ 24.  Under _Lopez_ , if the State makes a prima facie showing of probable cause at the preliminary hearing, then the Defendant may renew his motion to continue. But the Court should only grant that motion if he can show that additional testimony “is necessary to present evidence on a specific point material to the probable cause determination, and that it is reasonably likely to defeat the State’s prima facie showing of probable cause.” _Lopez_ , 2020 UT 

5 

61, ¶ 54. If Defendant cannot make that showing, the Court should deny the motion to continue and the preliminary hearing should close. 

The Court should note that the evidence the State anticipates consists of more than just the 

expert reports which are the basis of Defendant’s motion. This provides another reason to wait until the close of the preliminary hearing to address Defendant’s motion. Aside from forensic reports, the evidence will include images of Defendant at the scene, incriminating evidence in his possession, and multiple confessions. Consequently, even if Defendant could show that expert testimony and additional expert discovery would defeat expert conclusions tying him to the murder, it would not defeat probable cause. 

Following the _Lopez_ analysis ensures that the victim’s right to speedy trial is not affected by an unnecessary motion to continue. It takes into account the limited purpose of the hearing, the burden of proof, and the principle that the Court does not weigh contradictory evidence at this stage.  It also ensures that, if the evidence defense counsel seeks is reasonably likely to defeat probable cause, he gets the opportunity to present it. Accordingly, the State asks the Court to deny the motion to continue the preliminary hearing at this juncture, and rule on any renewed motion to continue after the State has rested its preliminary hearing evidence. 

# **2. Defendant’s analysis puts the cart before the horse.** 

Defendant’s argument that the preliminary hearing cannot take place “without full and complete discovery of all information underlying any forensic analysis,” Def. Mot. 17, and a full opportunity to attack expert opinions, _id._ at 2, misunderstands the limited purpose of the 

6 

preliminary hearing and discovery rights as they apply at this stage. In addition, his position would 

create chaos in practice. 

# **2.1. The constitution does not require either complete discovery or the opportunity to fully challenge expert testimony at the preliminary hearing.** 

Defendant cites the Utah Constitution which allows the use of reliable hearsay, “if appropriate discovery is _allowed_ as defined by statute or rule.” Def. Mot. 10, citing Art. 1, Sec. 12 (emphasis added). He also cites Rule 16 to argue that the Utah Constitution should be interpreted to mean that all material in the possession of law enforcement, including law enforcement forensic experts, must be provided prior to the preliminary hearing. Def. Mot. 10-11. But Defendant misunderstands the nature of the preliminary hearing and the rules governing discovery. 

# **2.1.1. The Court should consider discovery obligations in light of the preliminary hearing’s limited purpose and scope.** 

As Defendant acknowledges, the preliminary hearing serves a gatekeeping function to 

determine whether the evidence “warrants further proceedings,” Def. Mot. 9 (citing _State v. Zahn,_ 2008 UT App 56, ¶ 3), which should take place “either before or promptly after arrest.” Def. Mot. 23 (citing _Gerstein v. Pugh_ , 420 U.S. 103, 124-25); _see also_ Utah R. Crim.P. 7(e) (preliminary hearing to take place within 14 days of the request). The preliminary hearing is Utah’s equivalent to the grand jury indictment. It is designed to be one of the earliest stages of the criminal process as a protection for the accused so that he does not have to bear the burden of trial and incarceration where probable cause is lacking. 

Because the preliminary hearing is an early-stage proceeding—designed not to determine guilt but to “ferret out groundless and improvident prosecutions”, _State v. Schmidt_ , 2015 UT 65, ¶ 

7 

19, 356 P.3d 1204 (cleaned up)—the level of due process required is far lower than at trial. “The findings of probable cause may be based, in whole or in part, on reliable hearsay.” Utah R. Crim. P. 7B(b).  By definition, “evidence establishing the foundation for or authenticity of any exhibit,” “scientific, laboratory, or forensic reports and records,” and “medical and autopsy reports and records,” are reliable hearsay admissible without the need for 702 or other foundation or testimony from the author. Utah R. Evid. 1102(b). 

The court may not weigh contradictory testimony and must view all evidence in the light most favorable to the State because those matters are reserved for the trier of fact. _Virgin_ , 2006 UT 29, ¶ 24. “A magistrate has discretion to decline bindover only where the facts presented by the prosecution provide no more than a basis for speculation – as opposed to providing a basis for a reasonable belief.” _Schmidt,_ 2015 UT 65, at ¶ 187. In other words, even if Defendant’s experts disagree with the State’s experts, which at this point is groundless speculation, it would not affect the probable cause determination. 

Important trial rights to not apply to the preliminary hearing. The right to confrontation does not apply, _State v. Timmerman_ , 218 P.3d 590, 594-96, 2009 UT 58, ¶¶ 13-15, subpoena rights are limited, _Lopez_ , 2020 UT 61, ¶ 37, and Fourth Amendment suppression rights do not apply. Utah R. Crim. P. 7B(b).  The preliminary hearing is not the place to raise constitutional challenges. _State v. Arghittu_ , 2015 UT App 22, ¶ 30. It does not serve a discovery purpose, _State v. Labrum_ , 2025 UT 12, ¶ 59, and it is not a discovery device to give the defendant notice of the evidence, facts, or theory of the State’s case. _State v. Williamson_ , 2024 UT App 141, ¶ 48. 

8 

Because the purpose of the preliminary hearing is so narrow, defense counsel does not have a similar motive for cross-examination as at trial, and, as a result, cross-examination of preliminary hearing witnesses does not satisfy confrontation rights. _State v. Goins_ , 423 P.3d 1236, 1242, 2017 UT 61, ¶ 37. In fact, in _Goins_ , the Utah Supreme Court went so far as to say that defense counsel could reasonably forego vigorous cross examination at the preliminary hearing given the hearing’s limited purpose. _Id._ at ¶ 34. (“A defense attorney who assumes that the magistrate will conduct a preliminary hearing that comports with article I, section 12 does not have an incentive to prepare to thoroughly cross-examine on credibility.”) 

Reading the rules as Defendant requests would displace the constitutionally narrow preliminary hearing with a much more robust trial-like proceeding which would go far beyond the hearing’s purpose. Contrary to controlling legal standards and precedent, Defendant’s proposal expands the due process rights afforded at the preliminary hearing and, like the defense proposal rejected in _Lopez_ , “would obviate the legal sufficiency of hearsay evidence.” 

# **2.1.2. Expert discovery is due before trial, not before preliminary hearing.** 

Defendant cites no authority establishing that expert discovery must be complete before 

the preliminary hearing. In fact, the law expressly establishes otherwise. Utah Code § 77-17-13 states, 

If the prosecution or the defense intends to call any expert to testify in a felony case at trial or any hearing, excluding a preliminary hearing held pursuant to Rule 7 of the Utah Rules of Criminal Procedure, the party intending to call the expert shall give notice to the opposing party as soon as practicable but not less than 30 days before trial or 10 days before the hearing. 

9 

Expert notice requires production of “a copy of the expert’s report” or “a written explanation of the expert’s proposed testimony,” as well as “results of any tests or other specialized data.” _Id._ Presumably, the reason expert notice does not apply to preliminary hearings is because of that hearing’s limited purpose. 

Complete expert discovery appears to be precisely what Defendant has requested here. In other words, contrary to statute, Defendant wants full expert notice now rather than before trial. Because expert notice requirements, including discovery of foundational materials, are expressly excluded from the preliminary hearing by the expert notice statute, Defendant has no basis for seeking a continuance on the grounds of allegedly inadequate expert discovery. 

# **2.1.3. “Appropriate discovery” means only all material in the prosecutor’s possession at the time of the preliminary hearing.** 

The language of Article I, Section 12, states that reliable hearsay is admissible at 

preliminary hearings “if appropriate discovery is _allowed_ as defined by rule or statute.” Utah Const. art. I, §12 (emphasis added). The provision therefore does not require that all discovery be “provided” to the defense before the preliminary hearing. Rather it requires only that “appropriate discovery” be “allowed” to enable the defendant to adequately prepare for trial and address any facts or inferences that were supported only by reliable hearsay at a preliminary hearing. _Id._ 

Rule 16, which governs discovery in criminal cases, requires only that before preliminary hearing, the prosecutor disclose evidence that is reasonably available to _him_ , not to the entire prosecution team. The rule distinguishes “the prosecution team” from “the prosecutor.” Rule 16(a)(1) states, “the **_prosecutor_** must disclose to the defendant the following material or 

10 

information directly related to the case of which the **_prosecution team_** has knowledge or control: 

…” Rule 16(a)(2), regarding timing of mandatory disclosures, states, 

The **_prosecutor’s_** duty to disclose under paragraph (a)(1) is _a continuing duty as the material or information becomes known to the_ **_prosecutor_** . The **_prosecutor’_** _s_ disclosures must be made _as soon as practicable_ following the filing of an information, except that a **_prosecutor_** must disclose all evidence that the **_prosecutor_** relied upon to file the information within five days after the day on which the **_prosecutor_** receives a request for discovery from the defendant. In every case, all material or information listed under paragraph (a)(1) that is _presently and reasonably available_ to the **_prosecutor_** must be disclosed before the preliminary examination, if applicable, or before the defendant enters a plea of guilty or no contest or goes to trial, unless otherwise waived by the defendant. 

_id._ (emphasis added). Rule 16 is further informed by _Brady v. Maryland_ and its progeny, 

particularly as it relates to trial rights, but the rule also sets out a practical standard applicable to the preliminary hearing stage: the prosecutor cannot provide what he doesn’t possess and what he doesn’t know about. That is why the rule states that “all material or information listed under paragraph (a)(1) that is _presently and reasonably available_ to the **_prosecutor_** must be disclosed before the preliminary examination.” _See id._ (emphasis added). 

This distinction matters because, at the stage of a preliminary hearing, investigation and expert discovery are often incomplete. Detectives and analysts may be continuing their investigations, reducing them to writing, finalizing them and approving them for dissemination. Rule 16 acknowledges this fact by recognizing that discovery is a continuing duty which requires disclosure “as the material … becomes known to the prosecutor,” and limiting the scope of completed discovery at the preliminary hearing to material “presently and reasonably available to the prosecutor.” Utah R. Crim. P. 16(a)(2). Furthermore, in contrast to the provisions of the rule which apply to trial discovery (requiring notice at least 14 days before trial), rule 16 gives no 

11 

deadline for preliminary hearing discovery.  Defendant cites no authority requiring complete discovery prior to the preliminary hearing. And he cites no authority interpreting rule 16(a)(2) as it applies to preliminary hearing discovery. Instead, he cites two cases, _State v. Shabata_ , 678 P.2d 785 (Utah 1984), and _State v. Knight,_ 734 P.2d 913, n. 5 (Utah 1987), which deal with police withholding evidence through the trial. These cases give no guidance related to the standards governing preliminary hearing discovery and they certainly do not compel complete discovery prior to the preliminary hearing. While the State agrees that it must provide all discoverable material in the possession of law enforcement before trial, the State disagrees that discovery obligations for _all material_ in the possession of law enforcement attach before the preliminary hearing. Instead, the language of rule 16(a)(2) governs. 

As an investigation continues, investigators (both experts and non-experts) document their work and provide it to records specialists who process it and provide it to the prosecutors, who review, redact, and Bates number the files and then provide them to the defense. That process is taking place regularly in this case. Because expert investigation will properly continue until an expert deadline near trial, the Court should understand that material “presently and reasonably available to the prosecutor” in this context means only investigation and expert material which has been finalized, processed and provided to the prosecutor to date. 

# **2.2. Defendant’s suggestion would create chaos in the criminal process.** 

If the Court were to adopt Defendant’s approach, the State would have to either (1) complete all expert investigation and discovery before the preliminary hearing or (2) delay additional investigation until after the preliminary hearing. This is an untenable position. It would 

12 

be both inefficient and prejudicial to all parties – conflicting with principles of judicial economy and speedy trial rights. Instead, the rules should be read to encourage early preparation, including expert investigation, even where that expert investigation and disclosure of that investigation will not be finished before a preliminary hearing. 

The fact that the rules expressly anticipate ongoing investigation and discovery, especially expert discovery, demonstrates the flaw in Defendant’s position. Indeed, “[a]t this stage of the proceeding the evidence required to show probable cause is relatively low because the assumption is that the prosecution’s case will only get stronger as the investigation continues.” _State v. Clark_ , 20 P.3d 300, 303, 2001 UT 9, ¶ 10. Of course, as investigation continues, so will discovery. As a result, the incompletion of expert investigation and expert discovery, by either side, is not a basis to delay the preliminary hearing. 

# **2.3. The** **_Longcrier_ analysis is not helpful here.** 

Defendant argues that _Layton City v. Longcrier_ , 943 P.2d 655 (Utah Ct. App. 1997), provides the proper analysis for considering a motion to continue a preliminary hearing. _Longcrier,_ however, dealt with a trial court’s denial of a defendant’s motion to continue _trial_ so the defendant could obtain legal counsel. _Id._ Ultimately, the court held that the trial court did not abuse its discretion by denying the motion to continue. _Id_ . at 661. 

In contrast, this case does not deal with either of the two important aspects of _Longcrier_ : the right to counsel (at trial) or the continuance of the trial. This distinction matters because the degree of due process to which the defendant is entitled varies based on the proceeding. This is true as it relates to the admissibility of evidence, the right to confront, expert notice, discovery, 

13 

and the consequences of the hearing. As a result, while _Longcrier_ provides a framework that may have some helpful elements, it is not the controlling test. Instead, the Court should follow the _Lopez_ analysis described above because it deals with the level of due process allowed specifically at a _preliminary hearing_ . Regardless, if the court did not abuse its discretion in _Longcrier_ when it denied the motion to continue the _trial_ to give the defendant time to hire counsel, it is hard for Defendant in this case to claim an abuse of discretion for the Court to deny a motion to continue the much less rigorous _preliminary hearing_ where Defendant is represented by capable counsel.

# **CONCLUSION** 

The Court should apply the _Lopez_ analysis here because it considers the unique limited due process rights which attach to the preliminary hearing, as well as victims’ rights. Under _Lopez,_ the Court must deny Defendant’s motion to continue because he has not shown that continuance will impact the probable cause finding. Granting the motion and continuing the hearing would not only

Delay also impacts the victim’s (and Defendant’s) speedy trial rights, the perception of legitimacy of the justice system, and creates a void of information which fosters distrust and baseless speculation. _See U.S. v. unnecessarily delay prosecution of this case but would also violate the victim’s speedy trial rights. Defendant may renew his motion after the State has closed its preliminary hearing evidence, but he can succeed only if he demonstrates that a continuance will allow him to present evidence which is reasonably likely to defeat that State’s probable cause showing. Otherwise, his motion to continue unjustifiably expands the purposes of the preliminary hearing. 

Defendant’s preferred analysis puts the cart before the horse by attaching late-stage rights to one of the earliest stages of the proceedings. His course would not only delay advancement of the proceedings but would delay the threshold determination of probable cause which exists to prevent needless incarceration for the lengthy period of trial preparation. Furthermore, if the Court were to adopt Defendant’s argument, the only way for the State to timely advance the case would be to pause all investigation (expert and otherwise) until after the preliminary hearing once the investigation met the probable cause threshold. This result is contrary to controlling law and conflicts with principles of judicial economy, efficiency, and speedy trial. 

For these reasons, the Court should adopt the _Lopez_ approach and deny Defendant’s motion 

to continue unless he can show it is necessary to a fair determination of probable cause.
