The law regarding public access to court filings has already been covered at length ( _see_ Dkts. 302, 337), so the News Media will not belabor the point.  But as a brief reminder, court filings are presumptively public, and both the U.S. Supreme Court and the Utah Supreme Court have recognized the public’s right of access to criminal proceedings, including pretrial filings. _See_ Utah Code Jud. Admin. R. 4-202.02(1), (2)(f); _Richmond Newspapers, Inc. v. Defense counsel tries to overcome the public’s right of access by arguing that the underlying motion “addresses evidence that has not yet been found to be admissible and is of a nature that it could easily taint potential jurors.”  (Dkt. 285.)  This argument fails for two reasons. 

_First_ , as the News Media has briefed at length, public access cannot be denied merely because evidence may later be ruled inadmissible.  (Dkts. 302, 337.)  As the State put it, “[T]he mere fact that a court has not ruled on the admissibility of certain evidence cannot be a sufficient basis for restricting access to a record discussing that evidence.  If it were, then most pre-trial filings discussing evidence would be presumptively protected.  But rule 4-202.01(1) plainly establishes the opposite presumption.”  (Dkt. 340 at 2.)  Moreover, the underlying motion does not even argue that the evidence it discusses is inadmissible—it merely seeks an order prohibiting further testing of the evidence and permitting a defense expert access to the evidence. 

_Second_ , the underlying motion and the evidence it discusses belie any argument that public access would “lead to bias and tainting of the jury pool.”  (Dkt. 378.)  The underlying motion discusses and attaches a four-page ATF report on a comparison between a bullet jacket fragment recovered during an autopsy of Charlie Kirk and a rifle recovered by law enforcement. “The result of the comparison was inconclusive.”  ( _See_ Dkt. 284, Ex. 2.)  Defense counsel does not explain how this evidence could taint or bias the jury pool.  Instead, the defense argues the evidence is _exculpatory_ : 

The bullet jacket fragment evidence in this case is material exculpatory evidence within the meaning of _Trombetta_ , and not merely “potentially useful evidence”

The evidence thus possesses an apparent exculpatory value within the meaning of _Trombetta_ .

The defense’s statement that testing “did not reveal incriminating evidence” demonstrates that the underlying motion should not be litigated in secret and that this case is inapposite from those where courts denied public access. _See, e.g._ , _Archuleta_ , 857 P.2d at 236 (denying access to documents discussing a “homicide [that] was committed in a particularly 

depraved manner, consisting of physical torture, anal object rape, and massive bodily injury to the victim prior to death”). 

For these reasons, in addition to those previously argued, the defense has not overcome the presumption favoring public access, and the Motion to Classify should be denied.
