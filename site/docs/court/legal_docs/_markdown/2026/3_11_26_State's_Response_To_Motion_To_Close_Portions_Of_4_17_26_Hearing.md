The State provides the following response to Defendants’ Motion to Close Portions of the April 17, 2026 Evidentiary Hearing (Dkt. 425). Defendant does not acknowledge, or therefore apply, the controlling legal standard. The State nevertheless does not oppose Defendant’s requests to close portions of the hearing that will discuss evidence of: (1) violations of the Court’s decorum orders; and (2) publicized personal information and the effect of that publication on the individuals whose information is at issue. The remaining portions of the hearing should remain open. 

## **ARGUMENT** 

- **I. Defendant does not dispute that the hearing at issue is presumptively public.** 

Defendant does not identify or apply the legal standard that should govern a request 

to close a pre-trial proceeding in a criminal case like a hearing on a motion to exclude 

media cameras and microphones from the courtroom. Mot. at 2-6. Instead, Defendant incorrectly assumes that the standard is analogous to the standard for restricting access to court documents, and then relies on his prior arguments explaining why, in his view, his motion to exclude media cameras and microphones should not be public. _Id._ But the rules governing access to court _documents_ do not govern access to court _proceedings_ . _See_ Utah R. Jud. Admin. 4-202.02 (governing access to “a court record”); 4-202.01 (defining “record” to include items like “documents” or “papers” that are “reproducible”). 

Whether a court proceeding may be closed depends on “the precise nature of the proceeding in question” and, preliminarily, whether a presumptive right of public access attaches to that type of proceeding. _Kearns-Tribune Corp. v. Lewis_ , 685 P.2d 515, 519 (Utah 1984). For example, it is well-established that the public has a constitutional “right of access to criminal _trials_ .” _Id._ at 518. And the Utah Supreme Court has extended that presumption of public access to certain pre-trial criminal proceedings. _See id._ at 512 (preliminary hearings); _Soc’y of Pro. Journalists, Utah Chapter v. Bullock_ , 743 P.2d 1166, 1178 (Utah 1987) (competency proceedings). But the Utah Court of Appeals has refused to recognize a presumptive right of public access to juvenile court proceedings, given the 

2 of 10 

nature of purpose of the juvenile court. _See In re N.H.B._ , 769 P.2d 844, 851 (Utah App. 1989). 

Defendant does not argue that a pre-trial proceeding on a motion like Defendant’s— 

to close portions of an evidentiary hearing on a motion exclude media cameras and microphones from the courtroom—should not be presumptively public. Mot. at 2-6. Nor is the State aware of any controlling case addressing that issue. Defendant has therefore waived any argument that the hearing on his motion is not presumptively public. 

Regardless, the Utah Supreme Court has recognized generally “the importance of public access to the criminal _process_ ” and “endorse[d] the presumption that significant criminal pretrial proceedings will be open to the public.” _Soc’y of Pro. Journalists_ , 743 P.2d at 1177 (emphasis added). “‘In addition to kindling public misperception and eroding public confidence, closure of significant pretrial proceedings perpetuates general ignorance and cuts off public knowledge necessary to a full understanding of the criminal justice system.’” _Id._ (quoting _State v. Williams_ , 459 A.2d 641 (N.J. 1983)). The Court should therefore presume, like Defendant, that the hearing Defendant has requested is presumptively public. 

## **II. Defendant bears the burden to demonstrate that closure is necessary.** 

Before a court can close a presumptively public criminal proceeding, it must follow the five steps “set forth in _Kearns-Tribune_ , 685 P.2d at 524.” _Soc'y of Pro. Journalists_ , 743 P.2d at 1178. 

(i) a closure hearing must be held, and that hearing must be open to the greatest extent possible; 

3 of 10 

(ii) if allegedly prejudicial information must be disclosed during the hearing on the merits, the court may close that hearing only after first attempting unsuccessfully to procure a voluntary nondisclosure agreement among the parties; 

(iii) the court may close only that portion of the hearing on the merits as is necessary to protect any countervailing interests, such as the accused’s fair trial right; 

(iv) if closure is deemed necessary, the transcript of any closed proceeding should be released as soon as it is possible to do so without prejudice to the interests that justified closure; 

(v) the order of closure resulting from the closure hearing must be supported by written findings and conclusions. 

_Id_ . (citing _Kearns-Tribune_ , 685 P.2d at 524). 

As the party seeking closure, Defendant bears the burden of demonstrating that closure is necessary. _See Kearns-Tribune_ , 685 P.2d at 523. To overcome the presumption of public access, Defendant must demonstrate that an open hearing will create a “realistic likelihood prejudice.” _Id._ The court must evaluate the likelihood of prejudice to Defendant’s right to a fair trial in light of the availability and efficacy of the various means available for selecting impartial jurors. _Id._ 

While the caselaw on closure of judicial proceedings discusses the burden of proof only in terms of prejudice to a defendant’s right to a fair trial, it does not appear to foreclose other considerations like personal privacy. For example, the _Kearns-Tribune_ court held that a court may close only “that portion of the closure hearing that the court finds to be necessary to protect _the countervailing interests_ , such as the defendant’s right to a fair trial.” _Id._ at 524 (emphasis added). For purposes of this motion, the State does not dispute 

4 of 10 

that personal privacy seems to qualify as a sufficient “countervailing interest[]” that could justify closure. _Id._ 

- **III. Closure is necessary only for portions of the hearing that will discuss (1) violations of the decorum orders; and (2) personal privacy and safety concerns.** 

Defendant asks to close portions of the hearing that will (1) discuss prior media coverage, (2) perhaps reference portions of the closed portions of the December 11, 2025 hearing, and (3) reference information that creates personal privacy and safety concerns. Mot. at 2-6. As explained below, the Court should close only those portions of the hearing that address (1) media reports that violated the decorum orders, and (2) information that implicates personal privacy and safety concerns. Defendant has not satisfied his burden 

with respect to portions of the hearing that will discuss already public reports. 

- **A. Only portions of the hearing that address reports that violated decorum orders should be closed; the fact that other reports are already public favors public access.** 

Defendant first contends that some prior media coverage has violated Utah law and 

this Court’s decorum orders and that additional discussion of this already public information risks reemphasizing reports that Defendant characterizes as “harmful and prejudicial.” Mot. at 2-3, 5. 

The State disputes that any media coverage has violated Utah law. The State also observes that the media coverage has not all been unfavorable to Defendant. Rather, many electronic media outlets and various other internet sources have used this case to advance various conspiracy theories. _See, e.g._ , David Gilbert, _Why Conspiracy Theories Took Hold_ 

5 of 10 

_When Charlie Kirk Died_ , Wired, October 29, 2025, <u>www.youtube.com/watch?v=M_DCrfVp3uU.</u> 

Defendant has not shown that it is necessary to close the evidentiary hearing to discuss media reports that are already public, other than those that Defendant alleges violated the Court’s decorum orders. Any violations of the decorum orders should not have occurred in the first place, and republicizing those reports would amount to additional violations of those orders. The State therefore agrees that portions of the hearing discussing decorum order violations should be closed. 

But Defendant has not shown that it is necessary to close the hearing to discuss evidence about the remaining media reports that are already public. On the contrary, the fact that information is already public favors maintaining public access. _See State v. Allgier_ , 2011 UT 47, ¶ 17, 258 P.3d 589 (holding that already public nature of information contained in court filing “greatly diminished” any interest in protecting it). 

_Allgier_ provides essential guidance on this issue. There, the Utah Supreme Court held that the defendant’s interest in a fair capital trial and sentencing proceeding did not require further restricting public access to a letter from Allgier’s fellow inmate, even though the contents were highly prejudicial and likely inadmissible, because, among other things, the information in the letter was already public. _See id._ ¶¶ 14-21. The supreme court affirmed the trial court’s grant of public access even though the letter stated that Allgier had confessed to capital murder and demonstrated his future dangerousness. 

Allgier was accused of aggravated murder and escape after he disarmed, shot, and killed the corrections officer who had transported him to the University of Utah Hospital 

6 of 10 

for an appointment. _Id._ ¶ 3. While his case was pending, Allgier’s fellow inmate wrote a letter to the trial court stating that Allgier had admitted to him that Allgier shot the officer three times—once each in the stomach, chest, and head. _Id._ ¶ 4. The inmate also wrote that he feared Allgier because Allgier had threatened to kill the inmate and his mother if the inmate revealed Allgier’s admissions. _Id._ 

The Utah Supreme Court affirmed the district court’s decision to grant public access to the letter despite Allgier’s objection that doing so would impair his right to a fair capital trial. _Id._ ¶¶ 5-6, 16, 18. Allgier faced the death penalty not only when the letter came to light, but also throughout the appeals process regarding access to the letter. _See_ Docket of Third District Case No. 071904711 at 31 (March 22, 2010 Notice of Intent to Seek Death Penalty), 78 (Oct. 3, 2012 Plea Hearing where State agreed to sentence of life without parole) (attached as Exhibit 1). 

The _Allgier_ Court held that the trial court properly weighed the competing interests and struck the balance in favor of allowing public access to the undisputably prejudicial letter. _See id._ ¶¶ 17-21. The court considered and rejected Allgier’s assertions that the letter should remain private because potential jurors “would be tainted by the sensitive and inflammatory information within the Letter, especially because the Letter would likely be inadmissible as evidence.” _Id._ ¶ 16. Instead, the trial court properly refused to restrict public 

access to the letter for two reasons. _Id._ ¶¶ 16-21. First, “given the amount and type of prejudicial information already in the public domain” allowing public access “would not undermine Mr. Allgier’s right to a fair trial.” _Id._ ¶ 16. Second, “Allgier’s right to a fair trial would still be sufficiently protected by reasonable alternatives to sealing the Letter.” _Id._ ¶ 

7 of 10 

18. Those alternatives included “the regular, time-honored processes for selecting jurors” like “‘an enlarged venire,’ a ‘thorough and searching voir dire,’ and ‘a detailed jury questionnaire.’” _Id._ ¶ 19. Indeed, the court concluded that these long-established tools of jury selection would be sufficient to protect the right to a fair trial even in circumstances where the balancing of the competing interests weighs in favor of denying public access. _See id._ ¶ 20. “These reasonable alternatives to sealing the Letter would provide sufficient protection to Mr. Allgier’s right to a fair trial in this case _regardless of whether the respective interests otherwise weighed in favor of or against_ sealing the Letter.” _Id._ (emphasis added). 

So too here. The fact that the media reports Defendant wishes to discuss in a closed hearing are already public favors allowing public access to the portions of the hearing discussing those already-public reports, especially where the Court possesses a robust toolchest to discover and address any potential prejudice when it comes time to select the jury in this case. _See id._ at ¶¶ 17-21; _see also Nebraska Press Ass’n v. Stuart_ , 427 U.S. 539, 565 (1976) (“[P]retrial publicity, even if pervasive and concentrated, cannot be regarded as leading automatically and in every kind of criminal case to an unfair trial.”). Defendant therefore has not shown that the competing interests favor denying public access to the portions of the hearing discussing media reports, other than those constituting decorumorder violations. 

8 of 10 

## **B. Any references to the December 11 hearing appear to be only to the open portions of that hearing.** 

Defendant also asks the Court to close portions of the hearing that “may” discuss “comments and events that occurred in the closed setting on December 11, 2026. Mot. at 

5. But as far as the State can tell, all references to the December 11 hearing in the motion 

to exclude cameras and microphones refer to events that occurred during the open portion of that hearing when the camera was turned on. The State therefore does not believe that 

any need to reference the December 11 proceedings constitutes grounds for closing any portion of the upcoming hearing. 

- **B. The Court should close the hearing if necessary to protect personal privacy and safety.** 

Finally, Defendant asks the Court to close portions of the hearing that will discuss private information that, in his view, was improperly publicized, and the effect of that allegedly improper publication on “the particular individuals whose information is at issue.” Mot. at 6. The State agrees that this portion of the hearing should be nonpublic. As the State has argued in the past, valid concerns about individual safety and privacy are sufficient grounds to restrict public access. _See_ , _e.g._ , Utah R. Jud. Admin. 4- 401.01(2)(B)(ii) (personal safety) & (2)(B)(iv) (personal privacy). 

## **CONCLUSION** 

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should close only those portions of the hearing 

that the State has identified. The hearing should otherwise remain public.
