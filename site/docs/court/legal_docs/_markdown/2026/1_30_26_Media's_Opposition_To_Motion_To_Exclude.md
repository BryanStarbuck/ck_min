# **<u>Preferred Disposition of Motion to Exclude Cameras</u>** 

On January 16, 2026, Defendant Tyler Robinson filed a lengthy motion styled “Motion to Exclude Still Photographers, TV Cameras, and Microphones from the Courtroom” (“Motion to Exclude Cameras”) [Doc. 305]. Defendant has asked the Court to seal that Motion to Exclude Cameras to ensure the public cannot see it. _See_ Mot. to Classify [Doc. 310]. Defendant has also filed that _Motion to Classify_ under seal. The News Media have opposed that Motion to Classify. _See_ News Media Opp’n to Mot. to Classify Motion to Exclude Cameras [Doc. 323].<sup>1</sup> 

Defendant’s request to seal that motion—and his request to seal his request to seal—creates an obvious challenge: the News Media is left to guess at what arguments appear in Defendant’s Motion to Exclude Cameras. In an effort to best serve the Court as it weighs Defendant’s request to bar all still cameras, all video cameras, and all microphones from the courtroom, presumably for the life of this case, the News Media offers the principle-driven response below, while reserving the right to respond to the specific arguments contained in the Motion to Exclude Cameras if and when counsel for the News Media is provided access to a copy of that motion. 

As detailed below, Defendant “[p]lainly . . . has the right to a fair trial.” _Press-Enterprise Co. v. Superior Court_ , 478 U.S. 1, 7 (1986). But as the U.S. Supreme Court has “repeatedly recognized, one of the important means of assuring a fair trial is that the process be open to neutral observers.” _Id._ Preserving media access to these proceedings while imposing safeguards through a decorum order is the best mechanism for protecting _all_ of the constitutional rights in play here. The Court has already worked diligently to strike that balance, and that work should not be upended now. The News Media asks that the Court deny Defendant’s Motion to Exclude Cameras accordingly.

# **<u>Argument</u>** 

Utah’s governing rules begin with a presumption: “electronic media coverage by a news reporter _shall_ be permitted.” _See_ UCJA 4-401.01(2)(A) (emphasis added). As the News Media has explained in prior filings, that presumption favoring electronic media coverage has constitutional roots, even if the specific protections for such coverage are now rules-based. Simply put, an “unbroken, uncontradicted history” of public access to criminal proceedings reflects a broad “presumption of openness.” _See Richmond Newspapers v. Virginia_ , 448 U.S. 555, 573 (1980). When news media attend criminal proceedings, they act as “surrogates for the public,” _see id._ , and electronic media coverage provides the surest route to conveying “what people in attendance have seen and heard,” _see id._ Defendant’s Motion to Exclude Cameras should be seen for what it is: a request that the Court set those important constitutional interests aside altogether. 

Again, because Defendant won’t allow the public—or even the News Media’s counsel—to view the motion through which he asks the Court to wholly discard that presumption, the world is left to guess at what arguments Defendant believes justify his extraordinary request. In a recent public filing on a separate issue, however, Defendant offered the following sharply worded hints: 

As the highly biased traditional local and national media and _nouveau_ international social media coverage of this case demonstrates, every in-court statement by attorneys representing the State, whether under oath or not, every statement by counsel for Mr. Robinson, and every observation and ruling by this Court are under a microscope and subject to micro-surgery by journalists, bloggers, media “experts,” and others. . . . 

Widespread publication of [images of Mr. Robinson] has served no legitimate purpose and merely fuels rampant unfounded speculation about Mr. Robinson’s state of mind during these proceedings. In the absence of judicial intervention to bring an end to this highly prejudicial pre-trial imagery and the resulting discourse in the media, Mr. Robinson is faced with choosing between his constitutional right to a fair trial and his constitutional right to be personally present at all critical stages of these proceedings. 

Def.’s Notice of Objection to Proposed Ex. 4.1, at 3–4, 7 n.2 [Doc. 330]. 

2 

In short, Defendant’s objections appear to center on the suggestion that statements made in open court—and even facial expressions or other nonverbal cues—“are under a microscope.” _See id._ No one questions the public interest in this case, and media outlets like the News Media have covered (and will continue to cover) this proceeding. But the public interest counsels _in favor_ of allowing meaningful electronic media coverage. _See_ UCJA 4-201.01(2)(B)(vii). As detailed below, with respect to any competing factors, including preserving fair-trial rights, the unique features of this criminal proceeding make electronic media coverage unusually _important_ , and Defendant’s Motion to Exclude Cameras should be denied accordingly. 

- **I. Rule 4-401.01 Governs Defendant’s Request to Cut Off All Electronic Media Coverage.** 

Because Defendant has asked the Court to restrict electronic media coverage, Rule 4-401.01 

of the Utah Code of Judicial Administration governs. That rule begins with a presumption: 

There is a presumption that electronic media coverage by a news reporter shall be permitted in public proceedings where the predominant purpose of the electronic media coverage request is journalism or dissemination of news to the public. 

_See_ UCJA 4-401.01(2)(A). In applying that presumption, a court may consider a number of enumerated factors, as well as “any other factors affecting the fair administration of justice.” _See id._ R. 4-401.01(2)(B). Because it’s unclear whether Defendant has addressed these factors in his briefing, the News Media identifies them here. 

**_Factors that may favor restriction._** The first four factors invite the Court to consider whether there is a “reasonable likelihood” of certain types of harm that could stem from electronic media coverage: (i) prejudice to the parties’ fair-trial rights, (ii) risks to an individual’s safety or wellbeing, (iii) risks to a minor, or (iv) unwarranted invasions of an individual’s personal privacy. _See id._ R. 4-401.01(2)(B)(i)–(iv). 

**_Factors that may favor access._** Two other enumerated factors invite the Court to weigh benefits of electronic media coverage: (vii) public interest in the proceeding (that is, its 

3 

newsworthiness) and (viii) beneficial effects of allowing public observation through electronic media coverage. _See id._ R. 4-401.01(2)(B)(vii)–(vii). 

**_Other factors._** The remaining factors are neutral. One invites the Court to consider whether it has “physical facilities” needed to accommodate electronic media coverage, and the other asks the Court to consider whether electronic media coverage will “create adverse affects” that are worse than other types of coverage—coverage “without recording or transmitting images or sound.” _See id._ R. 4-401.01(2)(B)(v)–(vi). 

To justify departing from the presumption favoring electronic media coverage, a court must find that “the reasons for doing so are sufficiently compelling to outweigh [that] presumption.” _See id._ R. 4-401.01(2)(A). _Any_ prohibition or restriction on electronic media coverage must be supported by “particularized findings,” and those findings must “relate to the specific circumstances of the proceeding,” rather than “generalized views or preferences.” _Id._ R. 4-401.01(2)(C), (D). As detailed below, any factors favoring coverage restrictions in this case do not outweigh those favoring access, particularly when accounting for the presumption of access. 

- **II. Defendant’s Request for a Blanket Ban on Media Coverage Cannot Be Squared with Rule 4-401.01’s Expectation that Coverage Decisions Be Made on a Request-by-Request Basis.** 

As a threshold matter, Defendant appears to seek a blanket ban on media coverage that would apply at _all_ future hearings. As the News Media understands Defendant’s request, the ban would apply no matter the subject of the hearing and no matter the nature of the coverage request. Put differently, Defendant is asking the Court _not_ to engage in a hearing-by-hearing, request-by-request evaluation of coverage requests. 

Such an approach would directly violate Rule 4-401.01. Coverage requests are made on a hearing-by-hearing and request-by-request basis. That is by design. _See_ UCJA R. 4-401.01(2). That setup allows the Court to fulfill its duty of making particularized findings relating to the 

4 

“specific circumstances of the proceeding” in evaluating each coverage request. _Id._ R. 4- 401.01(2)(D). And such findings can support prohibition or restriction of electronic media coverage only where “the reasons for doing so are sufficiently compelling to outweigh the presumption” favoring access. _Id._ R.4-401.01(2)(A). 

Though Defendant has so far demanded that his coverage-ban arguments be kept secret, Defendant’s argument appears to center on the volume of news coverage, _see_ Mot. to Classify [Doc. 310], and the fact that members of the public—that is, those who _consume_ accurate news coverage and then offer their own commentary on it—have engaged in “rampant unfounded speculation about Mr. Robinson’s state of mind during these proceedings,” _see_ Obj. to Proposed Ex. 4.1, at 7 n.2 [Doc. 330]. While the public’s interest in this proceeding may frustrate Defendant, and while the defense may view public commentary on media coverage as speculative or inaccurate, generalized grievances about misinformed commentators cannot overcome the presumption favoring coverage. That’s particularly true because the very thing Defendants appears to complain about—the public’s intense interest in this proceeding—enjoys substantial Constitutional protection. _See Craig v. Harney_ , 331 U.S. 367, 374 (1947) (“What transpires in the court room is public property.”); _Richmond Newspapers_ , 448 U.S. at 580 (1980) (holding “that the right to attend criminal trials is implicit in the guarantees of the First Amendment” (footnote omitted)). 

It is impossible for the Court—or for anyone else—to know now the circumstances underlying every future hearing in this case, much less at trial. As a result, the Court cannot make the particularized determinations the rule requires for _future_ hearings. Indeed, doing so would be akin to issuing a standing advisory opinion on unripe requests for media coverage that may never happen, or if they do, may be subject to different considerations and parameters. If Defendant intends to object to coverage requests at every hearing—in writing or by oral motion—that is his right.

But Utah’s rules do not allow Defendant to short-circuit that process by asking the Court to issue an improper standing order for hypothetical future events.<sup>2</sup>

- **IV. Electronic Media Coverage Will Promote Public Interest and Is Consistent with the Constitutional Rights that Utah’s Media-Coverage Rules Are Meant to Safeguard.** 

The News Media expects that Defendant’s motion devotes little attention to the interests that 

_favor_ media access, to the extent he mentions them at all. But those interests are critical, and they are grounded in fundamental constitutional rights. The presumption of openness associated with criminal proceedings is not controversial. _See Richmond Newspapers_ , 448 U.S. at 555 (“From this unbroken, uncontradicted history, supported by reasons as valid today as in centuries past, we are bound to conclude that a presumption of openness inheres in the very nature of a criminal trial under our system of justice.”).<sup>6</sup> 

That presumption of openness exists because open courts foster and protect important societal values. Access enhances the quality and safeguards the integrity of the fact-finding process, _Globe Newspaper Co._ , 457 U.S. at 604, enhances basic fairness and the appearance of fairness in the proceedings, _Press-Enterprise_ , 478 U.S. at 13, fosters public confidence in the judicial process and acceptance of its results, _Press-Enterprise_ , 464 U.S. at 506, acts as a check on the judiciary, _Richmond Newspapers_ , 448 U.S. at 568, and allows the public to participate in government, _id._ at 587 (Brennan, J., concurring). All of those interests are enhanced by allowing the public to more fully “attend” court proceedings. In our modern era, that “attendance” is best facilitated by

# **<u>Conclusion</u>** 

The First Amendment provides a right of meaningful access, so courts are “obligated to take every reasonable measure to accommodate public attendance at criminal [proceedings].” _Presley v. Georgia_ , 558 U.S. 209, 215 (2010). Utah’s rules contain a presumption favoring media access— including still photos and video and sound recording—because that presumption is consistent with the general, well-established presumption of openness that inheres in any criminal proceeding. To the extent restrictions on media coverage are needed in this case, those restrictions can—and 

11 

should—be applied through specific coverage requirements imposed on media organizations that submit coverage requests, not by imposing a blanket ban on electronic media coverage.

Motion to Exclude Cameras accordingly.
