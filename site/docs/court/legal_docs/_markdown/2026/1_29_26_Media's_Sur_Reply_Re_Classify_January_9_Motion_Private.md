# **1.** **_Gannett Co. v. DePasquale_** 

_First_ , Defendant cites the 1979 case of _Gannett Co. v. DePasquale_ , 443 U.S. 368 (1979), and asserts (at 3) that it “is more analogous to the circumstances here” than the Supreme Court’s subsequent and extensive authority on the First Amendment right of access.  That assertion is puzzling, to say the least. _Gannett_ was not a First Amendment access case.  It dealt instead with the Public Trial clause of the Sixth Amendment, which guarantees a defendant the right to a public trial. _Id._ at 379.  The issue was whether the news media or the public, as opposed to the defendant, could invoke the Public Trial clause to obtain access to closed hearings. _Id._ at 38283.  In a fractured plurality opinion, the Court held that the Public Trial clause of the Sixth Amendment is for the benefit of the defendant, and the public does not have any independent right to assert it to obtain access. _Id._ ; _see also Ark. Television Co. v. Tedder_ , 662 S.W.2d 174, 175 (Ark. 1983) (“The only clear holding agreed on by a majority of the [ _Gannett_ ] Court was a finding that the _sixth amendment_ did not provide the public with a constitutional right of access to pretrial suppression hearings.” (emphasis added)). 

As for the First Amendment, contrary to Defendant’s implication, the Court _did not even reach that question_ , noting old precedent holding there was no such right, but stating “we do not decide” whether the First Amendment provides a right of access, much less what such a right would look like or the test to overcome it. _Gannett_ , 443 U.S. at 392. 

Which makes sense, because _Gannett_ was decided in 1979, a year _before_ the Court first expressly recognized the public’s First Amendment right of access.  In the term immediately

# **2.** **_State v. Archuleta_** 

_Second_ , Defendant cites _State v. Archuleta_ , 857 P.2d 234 (Utah 1993), and asserts (at 4) it is “[c]onsistent with” _Gannett_ and “instructive here.”  But because _Archuleta_ actually _did_ 

concern the public’s right of access under the First Amendment and the Utah Constitution, it has nothing to do with the Sixth Amendment issue in _Gannett_ .  And Defendant’s invocation of that case is equally puzzling given its full-throated recognition of the public’s constitutional right of access to pretrial documents filed in court proceedings. _Id._ at 238-39.  Defendant is correct that the _Archuleta_ court affirmed the district court’s denial of access to certain specific evidentiary documents, albeit on different grounds, and because the privilege is, of course, qualified.  But Defendant does not say anything about _why_ that conclusion was reached in that case on its unique and unusual facts, nor does he attempt to equate them to the subject matter of Defendant’s motion here.  The records restricted in _Archuleta_ contained “gruesome” and “horrific” details about a “homicide [that] was committed in a particularly depraved manner, consisting of physical torture, anal object rape, and massive bodily injury to the victim prior to death,” as well as “information concerning sodomy, physical torture to the victim’s testicles with battery cables, and anal penetration with a tire iron.” _Id._ at 236, 239 & n.21. 

Because the defense has refused to provide the News Media with their underlying 

motion, the News Media (and the public) are left to guess what the supposed evidence is that

To put a sharper point on it, no one has ever argued the public’s constitutional right of 

access is absolute and can never, even in rare or unusual circumstances, be overcome.  It is a qualified right.  But Defendant’s attempt to spin _Archuleta_ and _Gannett_ into a categorical argument that the public has _no_ right of access to any pretrial suppression motions or the

# **3.** **_United States v. McVeigh_** 

_Third_ , Defendant cites _United States v. McVeigh_ , 119 F.3d 806 (10th Cir. 1997) (per 

curiam), for the assertion (at 5) that the public has no right “to inadmissible evidence.”  But Defendant again does not explain what was actually at issue in _McVeigh_ .  That case does not support Defendant’s argument.<sup>2</sup> 

As a threshold matter, the question in _McVeigh_ was not primarily access to pretrial suppression hearings or associated motion papers, as Defendant incorrectly implies.  The question, instead, was whether the public had a right to access _underlying evidence_ that was excluded (and discussed in redacted portions of the publicly filed papers) after an open 

suppression hearing had been held in the full light of day.  Indeed, in the same breath, the Tenth Circuit explicitly noted that were it to need to decide the issue, the _Press-Enterprise II_ test would be satisfied for suppression motions:

Because the suppression hearing is the point in the process where the conduct of law enforcement officers is at issue, the public interest in access to a suppression hearing is particularly high. _Id._ Thus, the _Press Enterprise II_ test of “experience and logic” suggests that the qualified right of access applies to the suppression motion.

Also, unlike _McVeigh_ , there has been no ruling in this case that the evidence at issue is inadmissible.  In _McVeigh_ , the court was reviewing _on appeal_ , under an abuse of discretion standard, the release of certain information where there had already been a determination that the evidence was inadmissible.  That posture is entirely unlike this case, where Defendant is merely asserting that certain evidence should be excluded.  No court has held that the mere _possibility_ that evidence will be excluded at the trial court level is sufficient to prophylactically seal all evidentiary motions until a ruling is made.  To reach that conclusion would turn the presumption of access on its head and permit every single motion to exclude evidence to be filed under seal, contrary to the very right of access noted in _McVeigh.  See, e.g._ , _New York v. Arroyo_ , 675 N.Y.S.2d 272 (N.Y. Cty. Ct. 1998) (distinguishing _McVeigh_ in part because “the redacted items pertaining to the suppression issue had been ruled inadmissible”). 

In addition, and perhaps most fundamental, the _McVeigh_ court allowed certain evidence to be redacted based upon a finding that “[a]ccess to the redacted information is not needed for a full understanding of the court’s decision on the motion to suppress[.]” _McVeigh_ , 119 F.3d at 813.  That was not only because “the suppression hearing itself was open,” _id._ , but also because reasons for suppression in that case did _not_ depend on the actual content of the suppressed evidence. _Id._ at 813.  Rather, the court’s suppression order was based on the circumstances under which the evidence was obtained—whether the defendant’s interview with the FBI was unlawful, and whether the notes taken in that interview were hearsay. _Id._ at 809, 814 (“Thus,

both the press and the public had ample opportunity to understand the _circumstances surrounding Nichols’ statements,_ and the reasons why those statements were deemed inadmissible against McVeigh.” (emphasis added)). 

In this case, although the News Media has unjustifiably been prevented from seeing the actual arguments Defendant is making, it is quite likely those arguments are based on the content of the evidence at issue.  Denying the public access to that information will necessarily preclude the public from understanding the nature of the proceedings and the basis for any ruling by the court.  Unlike _McVeigh_ , there is no way for the public to have a “full understanding of the court’s decision on the motion to suppress” without knowing what the evidence at issue is. _Id._ at 813.  This case is much more like other cases where content-based evidentiary motions have not justified closure, and the public has been granted access to evidence despite its inadmissibility.<sup>3</sup> 

In any event, the _McVeigh_ court’s language regarding underlying, inadmissible evidence on appeal provides no authority for keeping motion papers merely _asserting_ inadmissibility under seal at the district court level.  Defendant has voluntarily brought an exclusion motion seeking a public court ruling presumably based on the content of the evidence at issue.  Having done so, he cannot complain that the content of the evidence is now subject to the public’s constitutional right of access.
