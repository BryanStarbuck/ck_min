Defendant’s Oppositions repeat generalized policy arguments against EMC that this Court has already rejected.  Those arguments are not sufficient to overcome the presumption favoring EMC and should be rejected yet again.<sup>2</sup> 

First, Defendant argues “there is a reasonable likelihood that [EMC] will prejudice the right of the parties to a fair proceeding.”  (Dkt. 717 at 2 (quoting Utah Code Jud. Admin. R. 4- 101.01(2)(B)(i)).)  He contends prejudice is reasonably likely because hearsay testimony will be presented at the September 1, 2026 hearing and because EMC “distracts and places added pressure on hearing participants.”  (Dkt. 717 at 2–3.)  This Court has already rejected these arguments because they “merely reflect generalized views or preferences” and could be made with respect to any preliminary hearing.  Utah Code Jud. Admin. R. 4-401.01(2)(D).  Moreover, the evidentiary portion of the preliminary hearing was open to the public and livestreamed, so the hearsay testimony is already available to the public.  Denying EMC now would only fuel speculation and erode trust in the criminal justice system.  As Defendant himself asserts, the “taking of evidence during the preliminary examination, oral arguments on that evidence, and the determinations made by the Court based upon that evidence is a critical stage of these capital proceedings.”  (Dkt. 717 at 3.)  That is true and exactly why meaningful public access to this stage of the proceeding is so important.

Defendant raises concerns about his counsel and individuals who provided 1102 statements for the preliminary hearing. And contrary to Defendant’s suggestion, the Court can (and has) addressed privacy concerns through narrowly tailored means and without terminating EMC.  Accordingly, this argument does not support any “particularized findings” that would support prohibiting EMC.  Utah Code Jud. Admin. R. 4-401.01(2)(C).
