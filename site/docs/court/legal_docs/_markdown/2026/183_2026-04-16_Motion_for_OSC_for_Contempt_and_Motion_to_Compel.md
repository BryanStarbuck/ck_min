### **MEMORANDUM** 

### **I. UTAH LAW GOVERNING CONTEMPT PROCEEDINGS** 

### **A.** **<u>Contempt Proceedings Are Controlled by Both Statute and Common Law.</u>** 

As the Utah Supreme Court has recently explained, contempt proceedings are governed 

by both statutory and common law: 

Utah law on the subject of contempt must be drawn together from case law and from statutes. The earliest law in this area is decisional and based on the common law. _See, e.g., In re Whitmore_ , 9 Utah 441, 35 P. 524 (1894); _People v. Owens_ , 8 Utah 20, 28 P. 871 (1892). The substantive and procedural rules were then altered by statute. _See, e.g._ , Compiled Laws of Utah §§ 1790, 1807, 6990, 7058-73, 7525-29, 9465 (1917). This legislation was not comprehensive, and our subsequent decisions seem to take the position that to the extent the common law was not inconsistent with the statutes, it survives and can continue to evolve. _See, e.g., Powers v. Taylor_ , 14 Utah 2d 118, 378 P.2d 519 (1963); _Robinson v. City Court ex rel. City of Ogden_ , 112 Utah 36, 47-49, 185 P.2d 256, 261-62 (1947) (Wolfe, J., concurring). 

_Von Hake v. Thomas_ , 759 P.2d 1162, 1166-1167 (1988). The statutory framework is now set out 

at Utah Code §§ 78B-6-301 et seq. In pertinent part, Utah Code § 78B-6-301 identifies 

disobedience of any lawful order of the court as an act or omission that constitute a “contempt[] of the authority of the court.” Utah Code § 78B-6-301(5). The “facts constituting” a contempt 

which does _not_ occur in the immediate presence of a court of judicial officer must be presented to the court through an affidavit,<sup>3</sup> and an “order to show cause” may be granted. Utah Code §§ 78B-6-302(2), 303. At a hearing on an order to show cause at which the person alleged to have

### **B.** **<u>The Elements of Contempt Where the Contemptuous Conduct Is the Violation of A Court’s Order Enjoining Certain Conduct.</u>** 

As to some acts or omissions which may constitute contempt, the common law generally 

sets forth _three_ elements which must be proven: 1) that the person cited for contempt knew what was required; 2) that the person had the ability to comply; and 3) that the person intentionally failed or refused to do so. _See Von Hake_ , 759 P.2d at 1172. 

However, the third “intentionality” element may not be necessary to a finding of civil 

contempt where the contemptuous conduct is, as here, violation of an order enjoining certain conduct. “The Utah courts have long recognized that civil contempt for violation of a court order or injunction requires no intent.” _Gill v. Gill_ , 718 P.2d 779, 781 (1986) (Justice Zimmerman, 

dissenting, quoting _Gunnison Irrigation Co. v. Peterson_ , 280 P. 715, 717 (Utah 1929)). This has also been the common law standard throughout the United States according to the United States Supreme Court: 

The absence of willfulness does not relieve from civil contempt. Civil as distinguished from criminal contempt is a sanction to enforce compliance with an order of the court or to compensate for losses or damages sustained by reason of noncompliance.  Since the purpose is remedial, it matters not with what intent the defendant did the prohibited act. 

_McComb v. Jacksonville Paper Co_ ., 336 U.S. 187, 1917 (1949) (internal citations omitted). The 

United States Supreme Court reaffirmed this principle just seven years ago. “We have explained before that a party’s subjective belief that she was complying with an order ordinarily will not insulate her from civil contempt if that belief was objectively unreasonable.” _Taggart v._ 

_Lorenzen_ , 587 U.S. 554, 561 (2019). 

Page **10** of **16** 

The United States District Court for the District of Utah has also explained that civil 

contempt under analogous federal law for violation of a court order need not be willful: 

In order for a party to be cited for civil contempt, a court must find that the party violated a specific and definite court order and that the party had knowledge of the order sufficient to put him on notice of the proscribed conducted. Nevertheless, the disobedience in a civil contempt need not be willful because the purpose of civil contempt sanctions is remedial. 

_In re Skinner_ , 90 B.R. 470, 472 (D. Utah 1988). 

### **C.** **<u>Burden of Proof.</u>** 

In a civil contempt proceeding, the burden of proof is one of “clear and convincing 

evidence.” _Von Hake_ , 759 P.2d at 1172.

- **II. THIS COURT SHOULD COMPEL THE STATE ATTORNEY’S OFFICE TO PRODUCE THE DOCUMENTS AND INFORMATION SOUGHT IN MR. ROBINSON’S DISCOVERY REQUEST.** 

After learning of Mr. Ballard’s recent and contemptuous media tour, Mr. Robinson 

informally sought from the Utah County Attorney’s Office documents and information which 

would disclose the scope of that offices’ recent contacts with media entities, copies of 

communications with those entities, and internal communications about those contacts. _See_ Ex. B. The Utah County Attorney’s Office quickly dispatched their objection. See Ex. C. 

Notwithstanding that objection, the documents and information Mr. Robinson seeks are 

discoverable both pursuant to Rule 16(a)(4) and in connection with the contempt hearing 

required by Utah Code § 78B-6-309.  Moreover, the Rules of Professional Responsibility require more. 

Rule 16(a)(4) provides that “[t]he prosecutor must disclose any other item of evidence 

which the court determines on good cause shown should be made available to the defendant in 

order for the defendant to adequately prepare a defense.” Utah R. Crim. P. 16(a)(4). 

The Utah Supreme Court has explained: 

Our Utah rule is a general parallel of the federal provision. Like the federal rule, our criminal rule 16 requires the government to "disclose to the defense" certain information in its possession. Utah R. Crim. P. 16(a). Under the Utah rule, the information to be disclosed includes "evidence which the court determines on good cause shown should be made available to the defendant in order for the defendant to adequately prepare his defense." _Id_ . 16(a)(5). And our rule also recognizes the authority of the court to enter sanctions for any failure "to comply with this rule." _Id_ . 16(g). Like the federal rule, our rule spells out specific sanctions that may be entered, but also states that the court "may enter such other order as it deems just under the circumstances." _Id_ . 16(g). 

_State v. DeJesus_ , 2017 UT 22, ¶ 63, 395 P.3d 111. 

It is also axiomatic that in connection with a contempt proceeding, if a Court is going to 

conduct the hearing provided for by Utah Code § 78B-6-309, it has the authority to compel 

Page **13** of **16** 

disclosure of documents and information that is germane to the contemptuous conduct at issue. The State cannot erect a firewall around documentary evidence related to its contemptuous conduct in order to defeat either a motion for an order to show cause, the show cause hearing itself, or the Court’s and the injured party’s assessment of the gravity of the misconduct and the scope of the sanctions which are necessary to remedy injuries or punish the contemnor. Moreover, the Utah Rules of Professional Responsibility provide that “[a] lawyer shall not: (d) in pretrial procedure …  fail to make reasonably diligent effort to comply with a legally proper discovery request by an opposing party…” Utah R. Prof’l Cond. 3-3.4. 

The Court should promptly compel the Utah County Attorney’s Office to comply with Mr. Robinson’s discovery request. 

### **CONCLUSION** 

After seeking and obtaining clarification of this Court’s _Pre-Trial and Trial Publicity Order_ , the Utah County Attorney’s Office has clearly violated those provisions which are designed to protect Mr. Robinson’s right to a fair trial. There can be no dispute that the Utah County Attorney’s Office, including both Mr. Gray and Mr. Ballard, were well aware of the conduct and statements which the Order enjoins, and that there is no legitimate limitation on their ability to comply with it. While a finding of intentionality is not necessary here, the decision to engage in media interviews and to send written statements to the press demonstrates the willfulness of the violations. 

This Court should issue an Order to Show Cause, should order the disclosures sought by Mr. Robinson, and should promptly schedule a hearing pursuant to Utah Code § 78B-6-309. Once the scale of the contemptuous conduct is more fully understood, the propriety of alternative sanctions and remedies will be better understood.

Thus, two questions remain to be answered.  First, did the UCAO exercise “reasonable care,” as required under Rule 3.8 of the Utah Rules of Professional Conduct, to prevent Sgt. Cannon from making extrajudicial statements to the media that violated Rule 3.6?  Second, if the UCAO did not exercise reasonable care, what sanction is appropriate? 

As to the first question, the Utah Rules of Professional Conduct and Utah case law are silent as to what constitutes “reasonable care” for purposes of Rule 3.8.  Defendant argues the phrase should be interpreted the same way “reasonable care” is interpreted in the civil context when assessing whether a person or entity has committed negligence.  In this context, reasonable care imposes an affirmative duty to act. _See B.R. ex rel. Jeffs v. West_ , 2012 UT 11, ¶ 7, 275 P.3d 228, 231. 

Rule 3.8(f) of the ABA’s Rules of Professional Conduct is almost identical to Utah’s Rule 3.8(e).  The ABA Rule reads, in relevant part: “The prosecutor in a criminal case shall: . . . exercise reasonable care to prevent . . . law enforcement personnel . . . from making an 

8 

extrajudicial statement that the prosecutor would be prohibited from making under Rule 3.6.” Given this striking similarity, the Court finds the ABA Rule to be persuasive authority. According to Comment 6 to the ABA Rule, “the reasonable care standard will be satisfied if the prosecutor issues the appropriate cautions to law-enforcement personnel and other relevant individuals.”  Thus, at a minimum, reasonable care requires some affirmative step to caution law enforcement about its duty not to make extrajudicial statements to the media which, if made by a prosecutor, would violate Rule 3.6. 

Before the first and second KNRS interviews occurred, the UCAO took no steps to train, instruct, or caution Sgt. Cannon about his duty to not make statements to the media which violated Rule 3.6.  Merely relying on Sgt. Cannon’s long history as an experienced public information officer does not constitute reasonable care for purposes of Rule 3.8.  This is especially true in a high-profile aggravated murder case like this one. 

Having found that many of Sgt. Cannon’s statements to KNRS violated Rule 3.6 and that the UCAO breached its duty of reasonable care to prevent Sgt. Cannon from making such statements, the Court must now determine what sanction is appropriate.  Defendant’s proposes four possible sanctions—dismissal of the charges, disqualification of the UCAO, change of venue, and enlargement of the prospective juror pool. 

When determining the appropriate sanction for misconduct, the Court “should be careful to ensure that any sanction is appropriately related to the nature of the misconduct and the resulting prejudice, either actual or potential.” _Wilson v. IHC Hosps., Inc_ ., 2012 UT 43, ¶ 94, 289 P.3d 369, 395.  The UCAO’s failure to exercise reasonable care to prevent Sgt. Cannon from making statements to the media which violated Rule 3.6 constituted negligence.  Negligent omissions are not of the same class as willful or intentional misconduct.  It is true that some 

9 

jurors on the venire panel may have been exposed to and influenced by Sgt. Cannon’s KNRS interviews.  But the prejudice arising from that exposure can be identified during jury selection and eliminated by the prudent exercise of challenges for cause.  The KNRS interviews do create a risk of mistrial based on too many potential jurors being excused for cause.  But that risk can be avoided by expanding the venire. 

For these reasons, the Court concludes that dismissal of the entire criminal case and disqualification of the UCAO are sweeping sanctions disproportionate to the actual or potential prejudice arising from the UCAO’s negligence.  Change of venue will not mitigate the risk of prejudice because KNRS is a Utah-based news and talk radio show.  There is no evidence before the Court suggesting that the radio station’s audience is limited to Utah County residents.  Thus, the sanction appropriate for the UCAO’s violation of Rule 3.8 is to expand the jury pool—which this Court has already done.

### **b. Conclusions of Law** 

Rule 3.6. of the Utah Rules of Professional Conduct provides: 

A lawyer who is participating or has participated in the investigation or litigation of a matter shall not make an extrajudicial statement that the lawyer knows or reasonably should know will be disseminated by means of public communication and will have a substantial likelihood of materially prejudicing an adjudicative proceeding in the matter. 

Comment 5 to the rule outlines the kinds of statements “that are more likely than not to have a 

material prejudicial effect on a [criminal] proceeding.”  These exemplary statements include, in 

part, statements expressing an “opinion as to the guilt or innocence of a defendant,” and statements about evidence that would likely be inadmissible at trial. 

The outcome of a criminal trial is to be decided by impartial jurors, who know as little as possible of the case [from extrajudicial sources], based on material 

12 

admitted into evidence before them in a court proceeding.  Extrajudicial comments on, or discussion of, evidence which might never be admitted at trial and _ex parte_ statements by counsel giving their version of the facts obviously threaten to undermine this basic tenet. 

Because lawyers have special access to information through discovery and client communications, their extrajudicial statements pose a threat to the fairness of a pending proceeding since lawyers’ statements are likely to be received as especially authoritative. 

_Gentile v. State Bar of Nev._ , 501 U.S. 1030, 1070, 1074 (1991) (internal citations omitted). 

Comments by prosecuting attorneys, in particular, have the inherent authority of the government and are more likely to influence the public. When such seemingly credible information reaches the ears or eyes of the public, the jury pool may become contaminated, greatly diminishing the court's ability to assemble an impartial jury. The defendant's right to a fair trial, thus, may be compromised. 

_Attorney Grievance Comm'n of Md. v. Gansler_ , 835 A.2d 548, 559 (2003) (internal citation omitted). 

Prosecutor comments to the media have a “substantial likelihood of materially prejudicing an adjudicative proceeding” if they are “(1) comments that are likely to influence the actual outcome of the trial, and (2) comments that are likely to prejudice the jury venire, even if an untainted panel can ultimately be found.” _Gentile_ , 501 U.S. at 1075. 

“In determining whether a lawyer's statements run the risk of materially prejudicing the case at hand, a court considers such factors as evaluating the statements themselves, the timing of the challenged statements, and whether the statements were published in the jury pool area.” _Underwood v. BNSF Ry. Co_ ., 359 F. Supp. 3d 953, 956 (D. Mont. 2018) (citing to _Gentile v. State Bar of Nev._ , 501 U.S. 1030, 1044 (1991)).  “Even if the passage of time, preventative measures by the trial court, and other factors prevent actual prejudice from occurring in a particular case, it does not necessarily mean that a prosecutor's statements did not meet the ‘substantial likelihood’ standard when made.” _In re Brizzi_ , 962 N.E.2d 1240, 1245 (Ind. 2012). 

13 

Here, the County Attorney called a press conference on July 31, 2019.  Media 

representatives attended and asked questions during the conference.  Clearly, the County Attorney knew that statements he made during the conference would be disseminated by means of public communication.  Indeed, he caused a video of the conference to be live-streamed to and later posted on the County Attorney’s public Facebook page. 

During the press conference, the County Attorney vouched for the credibility of the State’s key witness, stated that the Defendant had treated the victims like “trash,” stated that the Defendant is the kind of person from whom the public must be protected, stated that the death penalty (even if imposed) will never actually be carried out, referred to the substantial cost of capital litigation on taxpayers and the unfairness of Utah County shouldering that burden in a case that had no meaningful connection to the County, and referred to corroborating inculpatory evidence that the jury would never hear. 

These statements are textbook examples of extrajudicial statements carrying a substantial likelihood of prejudicing the adjudication of this case.  This is especially true because of who made the statements and when he did so.  The comments during the press conference were not the “off-the-cuff” remarks of some unknown assistant prosecutor.  Rather, they were statements of the County Attorney himself, an elected official at the highest levels of the criminal justice system whose representations are very likely accepted by much of the public as authoritative. Finally, the statements were made at a critical moment of the litigation—in the very conference at which the County Attorney announced to the public his decision that the State would seek the death penalty in this case. 

For all of these reasons, the Court concludes that the County Attorney’s press conference statements, set out above, violated Rule 3.6.  The remaining question is what sanction should be 

14 

imposed by the Court.  Defendant proposes five possible sanctions—dismissal of the case, disqualification of the County Attorney, disqualification of the UCAO, change of venue, and enlarging the jury venire. 

When determining the appropriate sanction for misconduct, the Court “should be careful to ensure that any sanction is appropriately related to the nature of the misconduct and the resulting prejudice, either actual or potential.” _Wilson v. IHC Hosps., Inc_ ., 2012 UT 43, ¶ 94, 289 P.3d 369, 395.  Here, the potential for prejudice is real.  It is likely that some people on the venire panel will have heard and been influenced by the County Attorney’s authoritative statements made during the press conference. 

Importantly, immediately after the conference, the Count Attorney—acting on the advice of his chief deputy who had correctly concluded that the County Attorney had violated Rule 3.6—walled himself off from further participation in the prosecution of this case.  The County Attorney did not reappear in any meaningful way in the case until recently when he retracted the State’s notice of intent to seek the death penalty.4  The decision of the County Attorney to effectively “disqualify himself” mitigated the prejudice arising from his press conference statements.  The decision permitted the UCAO to proceed forward in this case, independent of the County Attorney’s supervision and influence.  Moreover, the decision was an act of selfregulation—a hallmark of the learned professions.  This self-regulation gives the public confidence in the integrity of the legal profession, notwithstanding periodic missteps taken by its members. 

On the facts presented, dismissal of the entire case is a sweeping remedy disproportionate to the nature of the misconduct.  The prejudice identified by the Defendant is a tainted jury pool. 

> 4 The Defendant does not challenge this filing by the County Attorney. 

15 

But this prejudice can be identified during voir dire and eliminated through the prudent exercise of for cause challenges.  There is a risk of mistrial because too many potential jurors may be excused for cause.  But that risk can be eliminated by expanding the venire—something this Court has already done. 

A change of venue will not mitigate prejudice arising from the County Attorney’s press conference statements.  The conference was attended by a number of media outlets.  There is no evidence before the Court that the media outlets communicated the conference to Utah County residents only.  Moreover, Utah County is the second most populated county in Utah.  It affords the Court an ample pool from which to draw an expanded venire. 

Given the County Attorney’s self-disqualification after the press conference, the Court is not persuaded that the UCAO should be disqualified.  From the date of that disqualification, the UCAO has prosecuted this case independent of the County Attorney’s supervision or influence. 

Whether the County Attorney should be disqualified from further participation in the case is a harder call.  In opposing this remedy, the State relies upon a Georgia case— _Williams v. State_ , 369 S.E.2d 232 (Ga. 1988).  In _Williams,_ the Court held that “[t]here are two generally recognized grounds for disqualification of a prosecuting attorney. The first such ground is based on a conflict of interest, and the second ground has been described as ‘forensic misconduct.’” _Id._ at 238 (internal citation omitted).  Forensic misconduct includes extrajudicial statements which may taint the jury. _See id._ “In determining whether an improper statement of the prosecutor . . . requires his disqualification, the courts have taken into consideration whether such remarks were part of a calculated plan evincing a design to prejudice the defendant in the minds of the jurors, or whether such remarks were inadvertent, albeit improper, utterances.” _Id._ at 239.  The State 

16 

contends that because the press conference was not part of a calculated plan designed to prejudice the jury venire, disqualification of the County Attorney is unnecessary. 

The Court disagrees.  While the objective of the County Attorney in calling the press conference may not have been to prejudice the jury venire, the statements he made at the conference were substantially likely to have that effect.  A member of the public hearing the authoritative remarks of his elected County Attorney made during the conference might (1) conclude that the State’s key witness is credible and should be believed, because the County Attorney believes her and because there is inadmissible evidence known only to the County Attorney that corroborates her testimony; (2) conclude that the Defendant is a person to be feared by the community; (3) conclude that the death penalty decision is not a weighty one because the penalty will never actually be carried out; and (4) resent the Defendant for having unfairly imposed the substantial cost of capital litigation on Utah County taxpayers. 

In considering the appropriate sanction, the Court gives weight to the fact that most of the County Attorney’s statements were included in what appear to be prepared remarks.  The press conference opens with what appears to be a planned speech by the County Attorney about the case and his thought processes in determining whether to seek the death penalty in this case. Prepared remarks—as opposed to inadvertent statements—merit a more severe sanction because, where there is preparation, Rule 3.6 violations can be more readily avoided. 

In conclusion, the Court is not persuaded that a calculated plan to corrupt the jury pool is the only kind of forensic misconduct warranting disqualification.  Prepared remarks by an elected prosecutor which clearly violate Rule 3.6 are sufficient grounds on which disqualification may be imposed as a sanction. 

17 

For these reasons, as to Defendant’s second claim for prosecutorial misconduct, the Court 

GRANTS in part the Defendant’s motion for sanctions.  Consistent with the Court’s prior decision, the venire is expanded.  Consistent with the County Attorney’s own prior decision, the County Attorney is disqualified from further participation in this case.  The Court DENIES Defendant’s motion to dismiss the case, disqualify the UCAO, and to change venue.

### **b. Conclusions of Law** 

Pursuant to Rule 3.3(e) of the Utah Rules of Professional Conduct, “[i]n an ex parte proceeding, a lawyer shall inform the tribunal of all material facts known to the lawyer that will enable the tribunal to make an informed decision, whether or not the facts are adverse.” 

Defendant argues that Mr. Hollan failed to disclose a material fact in the second warrant application—the fact that police had already seized the burnt clothing that was the subject of the search warrant. In ex parte proceedings, lawyers have an ethical duty to inform the tribunal of material facts known to the lawyer that will enable the tribunal to make an informed decision, even when those facts are adverse to the lawyer’s case.  Here, the fact that the police had already seized the burnt clothing was material to the second warrant application, but only in the sense that it disclosed a more complete picture of what had actually occurred during service of the first

warrant and what would actually happen upon issuance of the second warrant. Prior seizure of the clothing was simply not a fact which would have enabled the reviewing magistrate to make an informed decision about whether the second warrant application should be granted or not.  Therefore, Mr. Hollan’s approval of the second warrant application did not violate Rule 3.3.

### **b. Conclusions of Law** 

Rule 3.8(d) of the Utah Rules of Professional Conduct requires that a “prosecutor in a criminal case . . . [m]ake timely disclosure to the defense of all evidence or information known to the prosecutor that tends to negate the guilt of the accused or mitigates the offense . . . except when the prosecutor is relieved of this responsibility by a protective order of the tribunal.” 

Defendant argues that Henderson’s mental health records may contain exculpatory impeachment evidence.  Since the date of Detective Pratt’s interview with Ms. Henderson about the defense subpoenas for her mental health records, the State has known that the disclosed list of Ms. Henderson’s mental health care providers is incomplete.  Therefore, the UCAO has a duty under Rule 3.8 to identify and disclose the identity of these unknown providers, whether by subpoena or by use of its authority under Ms. Henderson’s plea agreement. 

In response, the State argues that it has never concealed the fact that the defense 

subpoenas for mental health records may be incomplete.  Rather, the State disclosed the entire interview between Ms. Henderson and Detective Pratt.  Moreover, the State has no duty to subpoena or otherwise procure the “missing” mental health records referred to by Henderson. 

32 

The question presented here is whether the prosecutor has a duty to search for 

exculpatory evidence that may be contained in mental health records possessed by a third party. The Utah Supreme Court recently addressed this question of duty in _State v. Newton,_ 2020 UT 24, 466 P.3d 135.  There, the Court held: 

Under the first prong of the _Brady_ analysis, a prosecutor must “disclose _known_ , favorable evidence rising to a material level of importance.” _Kyles v. Whitley_ , 514 U.S. 419, 438 (1995) (emphasis added).  This, in turn, requires a prosecutor to “learn of any favorable evidence _known_ to the others acting on the government's behalf in the case, including the police.” _Id._ at 437 (emphasis added). But a prosecutor generally has no duty “to search for exculpatory evidence, conduct tests, or exhaustively pursue every angle on a case.” _State v. Shaffer_ , 725 P.2d 1301, 1305–06 (Utah 1986) (citation omitted) (holding that the cremation of the victim's body before gunshot-residue tests were performed was not a _Brady_ violation when “evidence of gunshot residue offered a ‘mere possibility’ of evidence favorable to the defendant”). Such duty arises under _Brady_ only when “the exculpatory value of untested ... evidence” is “apparent.” _State v. Bakalov_ , 1999 UT 45, ¶¶ 49–50, 979 P.2d 799 (holding that there was no _Brady_ violation when the State did not test a semen sample); _see also Arizona v. Youngblood_ , 488 U.S. 51, 56–58 (1988) (holding that the police's failure to perform tests on semen samples did not violate the Due Process Clause, absent bad faith); _People ex rel. Gallagher v. Dist. Court In & For Arapahoe Cty._ , 656 P.2d 1287, 1291–92 (Colo. 1983) (holding that the police's failure to conduct a trace-metal test on victim's hands before burial was a suppression of evidence when it was “implausible” that the “test had no value”). 

_Newton,_ 2020 UT 24, ¶ 39 (internal parallel United States Supreme Court citations and footnote omitted). 

Defendant has failed to prove that the “missing” mental health care providers constitute evidence “known to the prosecutor that tends to negate the guilt of the accused or mitigates the offense.”  And Defendant has failed to prove that the exculpatory value of these missing records is “apparent.” The mere possibility that the records may contain exculpatory evidence does not give rise to a legal duty on the part of the prosecutor to go in search of the records.  The State 

33 

disclosed Ms. Henderson’s belief that the defense subpoenas for her mental health records may be incomplete and it has no legal duty under _Brady_ or Rule 3.8 to do more. 

For these reasons, as to Defendant’s eighth claim for prosecutorial misconduct, the Court 

DENIES Defendant’s motion for sanctions. 

### **ORDER** 

For the foregoing reasons, the Court GRANTS IN PART and DENIES IN PART 

Defendant’s Motion for Sanctions Re: Prosecutorial Misconduct.  This is the Order of the Court 

for the matters addressed herein.  No further Order is necessary.
