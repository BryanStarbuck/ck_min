# **INTRODUCTION** 

The 181-page Motion to Exclude devotes pages upon pages to critiquing “the 

media,” ruminating on statements by public officials, and surmising that various officials and commentators violated ethical rules and even the law.  Those topics are a distraction from the narrow issue before the Court: whether the defense has made a non-speculative, specific showing that is sufficiently compelling to overcome the presumption favoring EMC.  It has not. 

A fundamental flaw in the defense’s argument is that it recounts the publicity surrounding this case before leaping to the conclusion that EMC must be terminated.  But there has been no showing of prejudice, let alone prejudice attributable to EMC.  Indeed, much of the publicity the defense complains of—including statements by the Governor and the President— exist independently of EMC and could not be addressed by terminating EMC. 

1 

The defense also refuses to recognize the benefits of EMC.  As has been briefed at length, the public has a right to access criminal proceedings.  This right “promote[s] an informed discussion of government affairs” and “ensure[s] the fairness of the criminal trial.” _KearnsTribune Corp. v. Lewis_ , 685 P.2d 515, 518 (Utah 1984).  EMC helps members of the public assert this right by ensuring that _all_ individuals can access the court proceedings.  Certainly, there will be those who sensationalize and share inaccurate information, but terminating EMC will not solve that problem.  On the other hand, EMC allows members of the public to see and hear the proceedings, giving them the most accurate information and the ability to discern for themselves whether other sources are trustworthy. 

Finally, the defense disregards the traditional methods for ensuring a fair and impartial jury, asserting this case is unique given the widespread publicity.  But Utah has had high-profile cases before, and in those cases, courts recognized the efficacy of the traditional methods.  Targeted measures like searching voir dire are the most effective for preventing prejudice—not blanket bans on EMC that attempt to control the public discourse. 

At bottom, the defense asks this Court to conclude that there cannot be a fair trial if EMC continues.  The 181-page Motion covers many topics, but it does not come close to making a showing that can support that conclusion. 

2 

# **ARGUMENT** 

# **I. The News Media Has Standing to Oppose the Motion to Exclude.** 

The Motion to Exclude does not argue that the News Media lacks standing.  But to lay 

any doubts to rest, the News Media has standing. 

Standing requires (1) an injury, (2) a causal relationship between the injury and the challenged actions, and (3) a request for relief that is likely to redress the injury. _Laws v. Grayeyes_ , 2021 UT 59, ¶ 27, 498 P.3d 410.  To show injury, “a party must demonstrate a ‘particularized injury’ ‘that gives [it] a personal stake in the outcome of the legal dispute.’” _Id._ ¶ 28 (quoting _Jenkins v. Swan_ , 675 P.2d 1145, 1148, 1151 (Utah 1983)).  The injury must be “distinct and palpable,” but it need not be a constitutional injury. _Jenkins_ , 675 P.2d at 1148.  The injury analysis ensures that courts are not “drawn into disputes that are not fit for judicial resolution or amount to generalized grievances that are more appropriately directed to the legislative and executive branches of the state government.” _Id._ (cleaned up). 

The News Media has a personal stake in the outcome of this dispute.  Rule 4-401.01 creates a presumption favoring EMC “by a news reporter … where the predominant purpose of the electronic media coverage request is journalism or dissemination of news to the public.” Utah Code Jud. Admin. R. 4-401.01(2).  Should the Motion to Exclude be granted, the News Media would experience a distinct and palpable injury not shared with the general public— denial of EMC coverage. _See Soc’y of Pro. Journalists v. Bullock_ , 743 P.2d 1166, 1174 (Utah 1987) (“[T]he requirement of adverse impact is met by anyone who claims a right of access to a judicial proceeding and then actually takes steps to exercise that right but is denied access.”). And because the Court is in the best position to rule on the Motion to Exclude, the News Media’s grievance is not one that is better addressed by the legislative or executive branches. _Id._ (“The issue—whether a constitutional right of access exists and is being denied—is of a type that is 

3 

committed to the judiciary for resolution.  Indeed, it is hard to imagine how the issue of access, especially access to judicial proceedings, could be resolved by any other branch.”). 

The remaining requirements, causation and redressability, are also easily satisfied. _See id._ (“In the context of challenges to court closures by those actually attempting to exercise access rights, [the second and third] requirements are easily met.”).  There is “a direct causal connection between” the defense’s request in the Motion to Exclude and the injury that will be suffered—the denial of EMC. _Id._ And the relief sought by the News Media—denying the Motion to Exclude—“would provide a direct remedy for the anticipated injury.” _Id._ 

For these reasons, the News Media has standing to oppose the Motion to Exclude.  That conclusion is not novel and has been the position of Utah courts for decades.<sup>1</sup> 

Separately, the News Media also has public interest standing.  “Utah law … allows parties to gain standing if they can show that they are an appropriate party raising issues of significant public interest.” _Haik v. Jones_ , 2018 UT 39, ¶ 23, 427 P.3d 1155 (omission in original) (quoting _Gregory v. Shurtleff_ , 2013 UT 18, ¶ 18, 299 P.3d 1098).  A party has public interest standing if the issue is of “significant public interest” and the party invoking standing is an “appropriate party.” _Id._ (quoting _Gregory_ , 2013 UT 18, ¶¶ 27–28).  Those elements are easily satisfied here. 

_First_ , whether EMC will be terminated for the remainder of these proceedings is of 

significant public interest.  That interest is demonstrated by Utah’s presumption favoring EMC,

# **II. EMC Is Consistent with the Constitutional Interests at Stake.** 

The defense spills much ink downplaying and attempting to discredit the presumption favoring EMC.  But the presumption is the law, and it complements important constitutional rights.  And although the defense relies on a “per se rule” against EMC in “notorious cases,” there is no such rule. 

# **A. EMC serves important constitutional interests.** 

As has been briefed at length, there is a constitutional right of access to criminal 

proceedings.<sup>2</sup> _Richmond Newspapers, Inc. v. Virginia_ , 448 U.S. 555, 580 (1980) (“We hold that the right to attend criminal trials is implicit in the guarantees of the First Amendment; without the freedom to attend such trials, which people have exercised for centuries, important aspects of freedom of speech and of the press could be eviscerated.” (cleaned up)); _Kearns-Tribune Corp._ 

> 2 For a more comprehensive discussion of this right, see Dkt. 220 at 5–8. 

5 

_v. Lewis_ , 685 P.2d 515, 518–21 (Utah 1984) (holding that under the U.S. and Utah Constitutions, 

“people have a constitutional right of public access to criminal trials and preliminary hearings”). 

Although there is a public right of access, it is obviously impossible for most of the public to attend proceedings.  Courtrooms have limited capacities, and regardless, most citizens are unable to attend for various reasons, such as the timing and location of proceedings.  These limitations present a serious threat to public access—“what exists of the right of access if it extends only to those who can squeeze through the door?” _United States v. Antar_ , 38 F.3d 1348, 1360 (3d Cir. 1994). 

Given these constraints, the press plays an important role in asserting the right to public access.  “Instead of acquiring information about trials by firsthand observation or by word of mouth from those who attend, people now acquire it chiefly through the print and electronic media.” _Richmond Newspapers_ , 448 U.S. at 573–74.  The press thus functions as “surrogates in asserting the public’s constitutional right of access.” _Kearns-Tribune_ , 685 P.2d at 522.  This “result[s] in far more general dissemination of information about judicial proceedings than the attendance of any other members of the public.” _Id._ ; _see also Richmond Newspapers_ , 448 U.S. at 573 (recognizing that the press functions “as surrogates for the public,” which “contributes to public understanding of the rule of law and comprehension of the functioning of the entire criminal justice system” (cleaned up)). 

Just as news reporting ensures that the public can access court proceedings, so too does EMC.  In fact, EMC increases the accuracy of reporting by allowing members of the public to see and hear exactly what transpires and to form their own judgments. _See Soc’y of Pro. Journalists v. Sec’y of Labor_ , 616 F. Supp. 569, 578 (D. Utah 1985) (“[T]he full flavor of the hearing cannot be sensed from the sterile sheets of a transcript.  Emotions, gestures, facial 

6 

expressions, and pregnant pauses do not appear on the reported transcript.”); _ABC, Inc. v. Stewart_ , 360 F.3d 90, 99 (2d Cir. 2004) (“[O]ne cannot describe an anguished look or a nervous tic.”).  So EMC works in tandem with the public’s constitutional right of access by ensuring that 

_all_ members of the public can access the courtroom and the most accurate information. 

EMC also complements the defendant’s fair trial rights.  The News Media has never argued, as the defense suggests, that the public’s right to access trumps the defendant’s fair trial rights.  Instead, the News Media has always maintained that EMC coexists with and even augments fair trial rights. _Press-Enterprise Co. v. Sup. Ct. of Cal. for Riverside Cnty._ , 478 U.S. 1, 7 (1986) (“Plainly, the defendant has a right to a fair trial but, as we have repeatedly 

recognized, one of the important means of assuring a fair trial is that the process be open to 

neutral observers.”); _id._ (“The right to an open public trial is a shared right of the accused and the public, the common concern being the assurance of fairness.”); _Kearns-Tribune_ , 685 P.2d at 518 (“Another reason cited for the First Amendment right of access is to ensure the fairness of the criminal trial.”).  That is not to say there are never situations where EMC can be restricted.  But 

the appropriate way to address those situations is on a hearing-by-hearing basis and with targeted restrictions, not through blanket bans on all EMC in all future proceedings. 

# **B. There is no “per se rule” against EMC in “notorious” cases.** 

The defense argues that the U.S. Supreme Court created a “per se rule” against EMC in “notorious cases.”  (Motion to Exclude at 58, 78–79, 81; Reply in Support of Motion to Exclude, Dkt. 399 at 8.)  The defense purports to find its “rule” in a clumsy blend of three U.S. Supreme Court cases: _Estes v. Texas_ , 381 U.S. 532 (1965); _Chandler v. Florida_ , 449 U.S. 560 (1981); and _Hollingsworth v. Perry_ , 558 U.S. 183 (2010) (per curiam).  None of these cases—considered together or separate—create any “per se” rules about EMC.  In fact, just the opposite. 

7 

**_Estes v. Texas_ .** In _Estes_ , the Court reversed a conviction based on the “considerable 

disruption” caused by obsolete technology and a dozen cameramen: 

[A]t least 12 cameramen were engaged in the courtroom throughout the hearing taking motion and still pictures and televising the proceedings. Cables and wires were snaked across the courtroom floor, three microphones were on the judge’s bench and others were beamed at the jury box and the counsel table. It is conceded that the activities of the television crews and news photographers led to considerable disruption of the hearings. 

381 U.S. at 536; _see also id._ at 550 (“In addition, the courtroom was a mass of wires, television 

cameras, microphones and photographers.”). 

Those concerns, while perhaps legitimate at the time, are not present here—there is a single pool photographer with modern, unobtrusive technology.  Also, the district court in _Estes_ “announced no restrictions,” differing from this Court’s proactive approach. _Id._ at 551.  And the _Estes_ Court explicitly recognized that its ruling was limited to the facts and circumstances before it. _Id._ at 540 (“When the advances in these arts permit reporting by printing press or by television without their present hazards to a fair trial we will have another case.”). 

The _Estes_ Court at no point created any rules about “notorious cases.”  Perhaps recognizing this, the defense relies on a concurring opinion.  ( _See_ Motion to Exclude at 81; Reply in Support of Motion to Exclude at 8.)  But of course, a concurrence does not bind any court. 

**_Chandler v. Florida_ .** In _Chandler_ , the Court made clear that _Estes_ did not announce “a constitutional rule barring still photographic, radio, and television coverage.”  449 U.S. at 574. And the _Chandler_ Court refused to adopt such a rule. _Id._ at 574–81.  The Court also declined to reverse a conviction on the grounds that it was televised, concluding the defendant had failed to show that the coverage denied him due process. _Id._ at 581.  The _Chandler_ Court did not discuss—let alone adopt—a “per se rule” regarding “notorious” cases.  And when discussing 

8 

state rules concerning EMC, the Court remarked that “the states must be free to experiment.” _Id._ at 582. 

**_Hollingsworth v. Perry_ .** In _Hollingsworth_ , the Court stayed the broadcast of a federal civil trial.  The ruling was based on procedural grounds—“the courts below did not follow the appropriate procedures set forth in federal law before changing their rules to allow such broadcasting.”  558 U.S. at 184.  The Court did not “express[] any view on whether such trials should be broadcast.” _Id._ 

_Hollingsworth_ makes no reference to “notorious” cases or “per se” rules.  Nonetheless, the defense argues that “ _Hollingsworth_ establishes that at least five members of the present Court believe that Justice Harlan’s concurring opinion in _Estes_ is speaking for ‘the Court.’”  (Motion to Exclude at 81.)  That is incorrect for the simple reason that only three of the current justices were in the _Hollingsworth_ majority.  But even if the entire current Court had signed on to _Hollingsworth_ , the defense would still be wrong. _Hollingsworth_ cited Justice Harlan’s concurrence _once_ , but a court does not adopt an entire concurrence as binding law by citing it once. _See id._ at 195.  Moreover, _Hollingsworth_ did not discuss or adopt any “per se” rules regarding EMC—it simply held there had been a procedural error in how a court rule was adopted, and it explicitly did not “express[] any view on whether such trials should be broadcast.” _Id._ at 184. 

In sum, there is no “per se” rule concerning “notorious” cases.  To the contrary, U.S. Supreme Court authority establishes that there are no per se rules concerning EMC, that states 

9 

“must be free to experiment” with EMC, and that a party objecting to EMC must demonstrate prejudice.<sup>3</sup> _Id._ at 574, 581. 

# **III. The Defense Has Not Overcome the Presumption Favoring EMC.** 

The defense devotes numerous pages to opposing Rule 4-401.01 on policy grounds. 

Those arguments are unpersuasive and irrelevant—the Judicial Council has long since adopted the Rule, and this Court cannot disregard it.  The defense’s remaining arguments are also unpersuasive.  The Rule plainly applies, and the defense has not carried its burden of showing the presumption has been overcome. 

# **A. EMC is presumptively favored.** 

EMC “by a news reporter” is presumptively favored “where the predominant purpose of 

the electronic media coverage is journalism or dissemination of news to the public.”  Utah Code of Jud. Admin. R. 4-401.01(2)(A).  As the News Media has already demonstrated—and the 

Court has already ruled—the News Media’s predominant purpose in providing EMC is journalism and dissemination of news to the public. # **B. The defense has not overcome the presumption.** 

To overcome the presumption favoring EMC, the defense must make a non-speculative, specific showing that is sufficiently compelling to outweigh the interests in public access.  Utah Code Jud. Admin. R. 404-01.01(2).  The defense has not—and cannot—make that showing. 

_First_ , the defense’s generalized complaints about publicity and “the media” do not demonstrate a reasonable likelihood that fair trial rights have been or will be prejudiced by EMC. The defense must show “a reasonable likelihood that _electronic media coverage_ will prejudice the right of the parties to a fair proceeding.” _Id._ R. 404-01.01(2)(B)(i) (emphasis added).  Put simply, there must be a reasonable likelihood that EMC will cause the alleged prejudice.  But the defense merely rehashes the publicity surrounding this case before jumping to the unearned conclusion that EMC should be terminated.

Similarly, the defense dwells on actions by public officials that it believes violated the law.  ( _See_ Motion to Exclude at 95–118.)  These alleged violations are unrelated to EMC and have no bearing on the question before the Court.  Lastly, the defense takes issue with how Robinson is “portrayed in the electronic media” compared to Kirk.  (Motion to Exclude at 139– 40). To hold that the mere existence of any preconceived notion as to the guilt or innocence of an accused, without more, is sufficient to rebut the presumption of a prospective juror’s impartiality would be to establish an impossible standard. It is sufficient if the juror can lay aside his [or her] impression or opinion and render a verdict based on the evidence presented in court. 

_State v. Lafferty_ , 749 P.2d 1239, 1250–51 (Utah 1988) (quoting _Murphy v. Florida_ , 421 U.S. 

794, 800 (1975)), _abrogated on other grounds by Met v. State_ , 2016 UT 51, 388 P.3d 447. 

This case will be tried by a jury—not the public—and the Court has tools to ensure a fair and impartial jury that “render[s] a verdict based on the evidence.” _Id._ Those tools include “use of an enlarged venire, thorough and searching voir dire, and a detailed jury questionnaire.” _State v. Allgier_ , 2011 UT 47, ¶ 19, 258 P.3d 589.  The defense brushes these tools off, contending the Court must “prevent prejudice, not merely [cure it] after the fact.”  (Reply in Support of Motion to Exclude at 7.)  But empaneling an impartial jury _does_ prevent prejudice.  And while the Court cannot control what every member of the public sees and hears, it can implement measures to ensure that the jury actually seated is fair and impartial.  These targeted measures do far more to protect fair trial rights than blanket bans on EMC that attempt to control online commentary.

_Second_ , the defense has not shown a reasonable likelihood that safety will be jeopardized. The defense’s main contention is that because of the publicity surrounding this case, there is a looming “danger of violence” that justifies terminating EMC.  ( _See_ Motion to Exclude at 155.) The defense has no concrete or particularized evidence of this alleged threat.  Instead, the defense relies on a linguistic analysis of statements by public officials and online commentators. (Motion to Exclude at 149–50.)  This analysis is tenuous, at best, and does not say anything specific about EMC—these same arguments could be used to support an argument (albeit a weak one) that the proceedings should be closed to the public. 

The defense also ignores the safety benefits of EMC.  When members of the public can “attend” the proceedings virtually, there is less likelihood of a rush to the courthouse to squeeze through the doors and vie for seating.  And, as the U.S. Supreme Court recognized, “the open processes of justice serve an important prophylactic purpose, providing an outlet for community concern, hostility, and emotion.” _Richmond Newspapers_ , 448 U.S. at 571.  Contrary to the defense’s argument, limiting access is more likely to interrupt this “community catharsis.” _Id._ 

Finally, it should go without saying that the comparison to Lee Harvey Oswald is misguided.  The News Media does not have—and has not asked for—“uninhibited access” to areas through which Robinson passes.  (Motion to Exclude at 154.) 

_Third_ , the defense has not shown a reasonable likelihood of unwarranted privacy invasions.  The defense asserts Robinson’s own privacy interests, but none of the cited authority concerns a defendant’s privacy interests _at trial_ .  ( _See_ Motion to Exclude at 158–61.)  That is unsurprising—a criminal defendant has no cognizable privacy interest in the subject matter of his prosecution.  Concerns about Robinson’s parents and unidentified witnesses are also unavailing. What happens in court is public and subject to reporting. _Craig v. Harney_ , 331 U.S. 367, 374

(1947) (“What transpires in the court room is public property.”); _Sheppard v. Maxwell_ , 384 U.S. 333, 362–63 (1966) (“Of course, there is nothing that proscribes the press from reporting events that transpire in the courtroom.”).  So the defense’s only argument is that EMC is more intrusive. But if that argument were sufficient, any criminal defendant could overcome the presumption by broadly asserting witness privacy interests.  That is not how the presumption works. 

The defense’s generalized concerns about publicity and “the media” do not show a reasonable likelihood that EMC will prejudice fair trial rights, jeopardize safety, or constitute an unwarranted invasion of privacy.  The defense has not overcome the presumption favoring EMC.
