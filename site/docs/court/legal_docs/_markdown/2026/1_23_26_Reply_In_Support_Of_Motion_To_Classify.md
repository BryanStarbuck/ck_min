# **I. The Initial Classification of the Motion Was Proper under Rule 4-202.04(3)(A) which Requires the Court to Deny Access Pending Further Proceedings.** 

The News Media objects to the initial classification of Mr. Robinson’s motion as private, claiming it was “improper.” However, as noted in the motion to classify, pursuant to Rule 4- 202.04(3)(A), upon the filing of a motion, “the court must deny access to the record until the court enters an order.” Dkt 285, at 2. Accordingly, Mr. Robinson requested that the Court classify the Motion filed January 9, 2026 and exhibits as private “pending any further order of the Court.” _Id_ . This is precisely what the Court did in signing the order on January 12, 2026, nothing more. Indeed, the Order states that the motion and exhibits are classified as private “pending further order from the Court.” Dkt. 295, at 2. 

Nothing about the order prevents the media from seeking the relief afforded under Utah Rule of Judicial Administration 4-202.04 and this Court’s _Order Denying the Media’s Request to Intervene, and Standing Order for Rule 4-202.04 Notice of Motions for Classification_ , issued December 29, 2025. _See_ Dkt 267. The notice order requires the parties to serve the News Media with all closure motions and motions to reclassify court records, and for that party to file proof of service with the Court. _Id_ . at 2. Thereafter,

# **II. Disclosure of the Potentially Inadmissible Evidence Has a Substantial Probability of Prejudicing Mr. Robinson’s Right to a Fair Trial.** 

The News Media is correct that Mr. Robinson’s request for classification of the motion is 

based on overwhelming concern that making public the evidence attached and described therein 

has a substantial probability of prejudicing Mr. Robinson’s right to a fair trial. At this early stage of proceedings, and prior to any adversarial testing, disclosure of initial evidentiary reports will only serve to insert potentially inadmissible evidence into the public narratives saturating the 

coverage of Mr. Robinson’s case and runs a high risk of tainting the potential jury pool. 

_First_ , although the News Media provides the Court citation to the Supreme Court case of _Richmond Newspapers v. Virginia_ , 448 U.S. 555 (1980) addressing the closure of a criminal _jury trial_ , the News Media fails to acknowledge and meaningfully engage in the Court’s preceding decision in _Gannett Co. v. DePasquale_ , 443 U.S. 368 (1979) that is more analogous to the circumstances presented here. The _Richmond_ Court emphasized the difference between the postures of _Gannett_ and _Richmond_ , stating: 

In _[Gannett]_ , the Court was not required to decide whether a right of access to _trials_ , as distinguished from hearings on _pre_ trial motions, was constitutionally guaranteed. The Court held that the Sixth Amendment's guarantee to the accused of a public trial gave neither the public nor the press an enforceable right of access to a _pre_ trial suppression hearing. 

Page **3** of **7** 

_Id_ . at 564 (emphasis in original). In addition, the _Gannett_ Court found regarding access to a pretrial suppression hearing, as summarized by the Utah Supreme Court, “even if the First Amendment gave a right of access it was ‘outweighed’ by the defendant’s right to a fair trial in that case, since there was a reasonable probability of prejudice if the transcript of a hearing whose purpose was to screen out unreliable or illegally obtained evidence was made public in advance of the trial.” _Kearns-Tribune Corp., Publisher of Salt Lake Tribune v. Lewis_ , 685 P.2d 515, 519 (Utah 1984) (quoting _Gannett_ , 443 U.S. at 392-93).<sup>1</sup> 

Consistent with this precedent and instructive here, in _State v. Archuleta_ , the Utah 

Supreme Court found the trial court correctly barred public access to materials filed in connection with a preliminary hearing, despite a presumptive right of access. 857 P.2d 234, 239 (Utah 1993). The _Archuleta_ Court noted that “the documents sought contained sensitive and inflammatory information that was not necessarily accurate or admissible.” _Id_ . After reviewing the record and the documents at issue, the _Archuleta_ Court concluded that “the documents contain inadmissible and possibly inaccurate information, publication of which would not serve defendants’ interests in receiving a fair trial” and “[g]iven the documents’ inflammatory and speculative nature, . . . a substantial probability exists that alternatives to closure would not have adequately protected defendants’ right to a fair trial.” _Id_ .

adversarial testing and may ultimately be inadmissible. _See, e.g.,_ Utah R. Evid. 702; _Daubert v._

The filing of the report was necessary to ensure the preservation of evidence consistent with the parties’ stipulated order, signed November 4, 2025, requiring Mr. Robinson to file an objection when put on notice that evidence may be tested or examined in a way that is “potentially destructive.” Dkt. 161, at 3. Moreover, defense efforts to exclude this evidence as unreliable would be futile if the 

evidence has already reached the jury pool through pretrial publicity. As aptly put by the Tenth Circuit: 

Neither tradition nor logic supports public access to inadmissible evidence. Access to inadmissible evidence is not necessary to understand the suppression hearing, so long as the public is able to understand the circumstances that gave rise to the decision to suppress. Moreover, suppressed evidence, by definition, will not be admissible at trial, and thus press access to such evidence will not play a significant positive role in the functioning of the criminal process, as that evidence is simply irrelevant to the process. On the contrary, disclosure of such evidence would play a negative role in the functioning of the criminal process, by exposing the public generally, as well as potential jurors, to incriminating evidence that the law has determined may not be used to support a conviction. 

_United States v. McVeigh_ , 119 F.3d 806, 813 (10th Cir. 1997) (per curiam). 

The Court should also be dissuaded of the notion that bias in the potential jury pool 

created by disclosure of evidence that may ultimately be deemed inadmissible will dissipate with time in this particular case. We no longer live in times where cases like Mr. Robinson’s are subject to a 24-hour news cycle, with each article or post easily replaced in the minds of the

public by a new story of the day. The appetite for this case is voracious and each new 

development, in or out of court proceedings, does not become an archived story, rather, it becomes a part of ongoing narratives that live and breathe on social media and the internet 

generally. The evidence at issue here will undoubtedly become part of any number of narratives and conspiracy theories pervading the public sphere and be repeated, _ad nauseum_ , up to and even during Mr. Robinson’s trial, regardless of this Court’s admissibility determinations. 

The only way to prevent potentially inadmissible evidence from becoming part of the 

narratives that lead to bias and tainting of the jury pool is to not file the potentially inadmissible evidence publicly.
