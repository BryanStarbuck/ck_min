                                          DISCUSSION

       The State makes several interrelated arguments in opposition to the motion to strike,

including arguing that the Sheriff has standing to join it in a pleading (that also makes affirmative

requests for relief) and that filing a joint pleading is essentially more convenient. These

arguments are unavailing.

       First, the State cites to cases discussing victim standing in criminal cases likewise cited

by Mr. Robinson in his motion. Opposition, at 1-3. The Sheriff’s interest in this case, unlike that

of a victim’s, is not supported by constitutional and statutory rights and this is key. For example,

in F.L. v. Court of Appeals, the Utah Supreme Court found that a victim had standing to contest

the disclosure of their own privileged records under Utah Rule of Evidence 506 and “under the

reasoning of State v. Brown.” 2022 UT 32, ¶ 36, 515 P.3d 421. The reasoning in State v. Brown

relied heavily on “[t]he right of crime victims to be heard in criminal cases” that is found in Utah

Constitution, article I, § 28, and by the applicable statute allowing victims to be heard during

particular hearings. 2014 UT 48, ¶ 13, 342 P.3d 239. The Utah Rules of Evidence alone did not

provide the victim standing; standing was afforded constitutionally and statutorily.

       The statute provided by the State, Utah Code § 17-22-2(1)(c) also does not afford a

Sheriff standing to file a pleading in a criminal case. This provision requires Utah sheriff’s

generally to provide court security and refers to administrative rules. This concerns an imposed

duty on sheriff, not any rights afforded to the Utah sheriffs. And, as discussed further below, Mr.

Robinson has never argued that the Sheriff should not be permitted to provide information to the

Court and in fact, specifically requested that this occur through the appropriate means.




                                                                                         Page 2 of 7
       Overall, the State still has not provided any constitutional provision or statute that affords

the Sheriff standing to file a pleading in a criminal case. The Sheriff is not a party and should not

be considered as a party by the Court.

noticed Interim Order from October 14, 2025, does not order or invite the Sheriff to respond. See

Doc 96; Doc 99 (Notification for Interim Order). The argument suggests that the parties should

ignore that the October 13, 2025 order was never docketed and noticed, and was promptly

replaced with a docketed and noticed order. In response, Mr. Robinson notes only that the

Court’s controlling order speaks for itself:




       Third, the State is correct that Mr. Robinson was required to provide the Order to the

Sheriff. See Doc 96. The State questions the utility of this provision if the Sheriff’s Office were

                                                                                         Page 3 of 7
not then invited to respond. It stands to reason that this Court, like the parties, determined that it

is not (and should not) be in a position to assume or argue and find standing on behalf of the

Sheriff. However, that is precisely what has occurred here, with one of the prosecutors drafting

and filing the opposition to the Motion to Strike on behalf of the Sheriff. Any guise that the

prosecutor’s office is and has been representing the people of the State of Utah is obliterated by

the fact that the State is now making a standing argument on behalf of the Sheriff. See

Opposition (signed by prosecutor Christpher Ballard).

        Fourth, the State also argues, in a footnote, that the defense observation that the legal

positions of the Sheriff and the State may be different and those differences may be conveyed to

the Court is “inconsistent.” Opposition, at 4 n.1. This is incorrect.

        Defendant has never claimed that this Court may not receive information from the

Sheriff’s Office regarding Defendant’s motion. In fact, Defendant specifically requested that this

Court issue an order for the Sheriff’s Office to provide information and for this Court to hold a

closed evidentiary hearing so that the requested information from the Sheriff, as a non-party, can

be appropriately provided to this Court. There is nothing inconsistent in or “illogical” about Mr.

Robinson’s argument that the Sheriff, a non-party, should not be joint filing pleadings with the

State, a party.

        Indeed, court orders, subpoenas, and evidentiary hearing are generally the means by

which the parties may request information from non-parties and present that information to

district courts. See, e.g., Utah R. Civ. P. 45; Utah R. Crim. P. 14. In responding to such requests,

third-parties are afforded the opportunity to assert rights and object as allowed by statute or rule.

This does not mean that these entities or individuals become parties to a criminal case that may

request affirmative relief from the court. Allowing the Sheriff standing to oppose a pleading and



                                                                                           Page 4 of 7
request orders from the Court governing the behavior of the Court and the parties sets a

dangerous precedent in this case (and for others) that should not be countenanced. Any

governmental entity with any interest in a criminal proceeding would be allowed to file

pleadings. This is an untenable result.

       Lastly, the issues before the Court are whether the Court should: (1) order the Sheriff’s

Office to provide information in order to conduct the individualized assessment required; (2)

hold an evidentiary hearing on the motion; and (3) while the underlying motion is pending, allow

Mr. Robinson to appear in civilian clothing and without visible restraints at in person hearings

such as the one that is presently scheduled for October 30, 2025, in light of the overwhelming

constitutional concerns raised in the motion. None of these issues require an uninvited responsive

pleading from a non-party—the Sheriff. To the extent the State desired the Sheriff’s position to

be considered at this stage of proceedings, the State could have properly provided affidavits or

other admissible evidence for this Court’s consideration.

       Given that the State could have provided this information in an admissible form, Mr.

Robinson acknowledges that this may seem like a semantic argument—it is not. A court’s

determination of standing is critical to its gatekeeping role and to the preservation of the

separation of powers. See, e.g., Utah Chapter of Sierra Club v. Utah Air Quality Bd., 2006 UT

74, ¶ 12, 148 P.3d 960 (“Standing is a concept ‘rooted in the historical and constitutional role of

the judiciary’ as one of the three and separate equal branches of government.”). In addition, the

State’s continued efforts to muddy the waters by arguing on behalf of the Sheriff solidifies the

conclusion, at least for Mr. Robinson and his counsel, that the State is apparently unable to

separate itself from the interests of a different government agency. Allowing two agencies—




                                                                                         Page 5 of 7
two parts of the executive branch—each apparently adversarial to Mr. Robinson to join in

pleadings together against him threatens very basic understandings of fundamental fairness and

due process.

       Thus, where the Utah County Attorney’s Office (“UCAO”) has not sought to distinguish

its own responses from that of the Sheriff’s Office, the pleading should be stricken in its entirety.

                                          CONCLUSION

       Where the Sheriff’s Office has not provided any authority affording it standing to file a

pleading in a criminal case, and where UCAO has made no effort to distinguish its own position

from that of the Sheriff’s Office, the Response should be stricken.
