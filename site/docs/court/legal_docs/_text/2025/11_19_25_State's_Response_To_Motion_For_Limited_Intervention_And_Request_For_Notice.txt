       Various media outlets (the Media Outlets) seek an order (1) recognizing them “as

limited-purpose intervenors in this case,” (2) requiring the parties to provide them with

“prompt notice [of] any filing or other request that seeks to limit access to filings, hearings,

or other aspects of this proceeding, including by providing a copy of any such filing or

request,” and (3) granting them an appropriate opportunity to be heard on those motions.

Mot. at 9. The State agrees that the parties should be required to serve the Media Outlets

with copies of the types of motions they have identified, and that Media Outlets should

also have an opportunity to appear and be heard on those discrete issues as limited-purpose

parties. But the State maintains its right to argue in favor of limiting access to filings and

hearings as it deems appropriate.

                                       ARGUMENT

I.   The Media Outlets may appear as limited-purpose parties to be heard on
     the discrete issues they identify.

       A person or entity who is not a primary party to a case may nevertheless appear as

a limited-purpose party and be heard on discrete issues in which that person or entity has a

direct interest when a statute, rule, or controlling precedent recognizes that right. See F.L.

v. Court of Appeals, 2022 UT 32, ¶¶ 35-39, 515 P.3d 421 (recognizing crime victim’s

standing to appear as limited-purpose party to protect her privileged medical records where

Utah R. Evid. 506 allows patient to claim privilege); State v. Brown, 2014 UT 48, ¶¶ 13-

20, 342 P.3d 239 (recognizing crime victim’s standing to appear as limited-purpose party

to assert claim for restitution where statute recognizes victims’ right to seek restitution);

State ex rel. J.T., 2023 UT App 157, ¶¶ 36-40, 542 P.3d 517 (recognizing grandmother’s

standing to intervene as limited-purpose party to request preferential consideration as

temporary kinship placement for grandchildren involved in child welfare proceeding where

statute requires preferential consideration of relative’s placement request); Soc’y of Pro.

Journalists v. Bullock, 743 P.2d 1166, 1178 & n.15 (Utah 1987) (recognizing right of

“members of the media and ‘other interested parties’ … to participate” in closure

proceedings “‘to the maximum extent possible’”) (quoting Kearns-Tribune Corp. v. Lewis,

685 P.2d 515, 524 (Utah 1984)).


                                              2
       As explained below, both court rule and controlling precedent recognize that media

members have standing to be heard on the issues that the Media Outlets identify. The State

therefore agrees that the Court should allow the Media Outlets to appear as limited-purpose

parties to address those issues.

II.   The parties must serve the Media Outlets with notice of motions to limit
      access to both court documents and proceedings.

       The State agrees that the parties should serve the Media Outlets with a copy of any

motion that seeks to limit access to documents, as Utah R. Jud. Admin. 4-202.04(4)

requires. The State also agrees, contrary to Defendant’s position, that the parties should

serve the Media Outlets with any motion that seeks to limit access to court hearings, as

Society of Professional Journalists requires. See 743 P.2d at 1178 & n.15.

       A person who files a motion to reclassify a presumptively public court document

must “serve” the motion on “any representative of the press who has requested notice in

the case.” Utah R. Jud. Admin. 4-202.04(4). If the motion is contested, a court must

“conduct a closure hearing” where media members who have opposed the motion can

appear and be heard. See id.

       Consistent with this rule, this Court has already ordered “that all future motions to

classify a record in this case must be served on representatives of the press who requested

notice.” Nov. 10, 2025, Order Requiring Parties to Serve All Closure or Reclassification

Motions on the Media (Dkt. 171) at 2. The Media Outlets have therefore properly requested

that the parties serve them with notice of “any filing or other request that seeks to limit

access to filings.” Mot. at 9.


                                             3
       Now that the Media Outlets have also requested notice of motions to close court

hearings, the parties should also serve them with copies of those motions. Utah Supreme

Court precedent requires “a party seeking closure” of a court hearing to “serve advance

written notice of a closure motion upon the opposing party, the court, and any media

representatives who have requested such notice.” Soc’y of Pro. Journalists, 743 P.2d at

1178 n.15.

       Contrary to Defendant’s interpretation, this notice requirement is not satisfied by

merely filing the closure motion with the court. See Def.’s Response to Request for Notice

of Motion[s] to Close Jud. Proc. and Records (Def.’s Response to Request for Notice) (Dkt.

150) at 3-4. The Society of Professional Journalists court required a party seeking closure

to give notice by both “formal” and informal means. See 743 P.2d 1178 n.15. “Formal”

notice is accomplished through “service,” while informal notice is accomplished through

“filing.” Id.

       The Society of Professional Journalists court recognized two categories of persons

who could be interested in participating in closure proceedings: (1) “media representatives

who have requested such notice,” and (2) “any interested parties, including members of the

public and the media.” 743 P.2d at 1178 n.15. The court then noted that “participation in

the closure hearing” is not limited “to those receiving formal notice.” Id. (emphasis added).

Filing a notice of a closure motion so that it appears on the case docket is sufficient to

satisfy the notice requirement for the second category of potentially interested persons—

who are unknown to the parties and have not requested “formal notice”—but it is not



                                             4
sufficient for the first category—interested persons who are known because they have

formally requested notice. See id.

       As to the second category of unknown but potentially interested persons, “[t]he

filing of a written notice of a closure will assure that those truly interested can make

themselves aware of the hearing and can appear.” Id. (emphasis added). They “will be able

to inform themselves of the pendency of the motion by the simple expedient of reviewing

the court file.” Id.

       But “formal notice” is required for the first category of known interested persons—

“media representatives who have requested … notice.” Id. For this group, as explained, a

party seeking closure must “serve” them with advance written notice of a closure motion.

Id. (emphasis added). Thus, while filing provides notice to unknown but interested parties,

service is required for those who have identified themselves as interested parties and

requested notice. See id. This requirement is consistent with the requirement for formal

service of motions to reclassify court documents under Utah R. Jud. Admin. 4-202.04(4).

       Accordingly, the State agrees that the parties must serve on the Media Outlets copies

of any motion that seeks to limit access to court documents or hearings in this matter as

rule 4-202.04 of the Utah Rules of Judicial Administration and Society of Professional

Journalists require.

III. Defendant’s right to a fair trial may supersede the media’s right to access.

     Finally, the State agrees with Defendant that “[a]ll participants in this proceeding have

a duty to ensure that [he] receives a fair trial and a reliable penalty proceeding.” Def.’s

Response to Request for Notice at 5. The State therefore also agrees that while there exists

                                              5
a “‘First Amendment right of access to criminal trials,’” that right “is not absolute and can

be denied where ‘the denial is necessitated by a compelling governmental interest, and is

narrowly tailored to serve that interest.’” KUTV v. Conder, 668 P.2d 513, 517 (Utah 1983)

(quoting Globe Newspaper Co. v. Superior Court, 457 U.S. 596, 606-07 (1982)). The State

therefore maintains its right to argue in favor of restricting media access when necessary.

                                     CONCLUSION

       The Court should grant the Media Outlets’ motion and (1) require the parties to

serve them with copies of any motion to limit access to court documents or hearings, and

(2) allow the Media Outlets to appear as limited-purpose parties to be heard on those

motions that they contest.
