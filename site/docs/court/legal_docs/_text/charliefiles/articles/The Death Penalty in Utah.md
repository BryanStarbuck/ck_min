# The Death Penalty in Utah — and What It Means for the Robinson Case

*State of Utah v. Tyler James Robinson* is a **capital case**: the State filed a **notice of intent to seek the death penalty** with the charges. This page puts that in context — how Utah's death penalty actually works and how rarely it is carried out, how long comparable cases take, and whether Judge Graf's pace is unusual — using **real, sourced cases and official statistics**.

> **Read this as context, not prediction.** Robinson is **presumed innocent**; his case has **not even been bound over** for trial (that is argued Sept 1, 2026 — see [[2026-07-10 - Preliminary Hearing Day 5|Day 5]]). Nothing here forecasts his guilt, a verdict, or a sentence. It is a factual look at the system he is in and how similar cases have moved, drawn from the **Death Penalty Information Center (DPIC)**, the **Bureau of Justice Statistics (BJS)**, the **Utah Legislature**, court records, and mainstream reporting. Every figure is dated because these numbers move.

## 1. Is Judge Graf taking an abnormally long time?
**Short answer: No.** Setting a briefing schedule and taking the bind-over decision under advisement — rather than ruling from the bench at the close of the hearing — is **within the normal range for a complex Utah capital case, and is essentially the default in Utah capital litigation right now.**

**What the rules require.** Utah Rule of Criminal Procedure **7B** governs the preliminary examination: the magistrate "must" find probable cause, execute a bind-over order, and "include any written findings in the case record" — but the rule **sets no deadline and does not require a ruling from the bench** ([URCRP 7B](https://legacy.utcourts.gov/rules/view.php?type=urcrp&rule=7B)). Rule **7** sets deadlines for *holding* the hearing, not for *deciding* it ([URCRP 7](https://legacy.utcourts.gov/rules/view.php?type=urcrp&rule=7)). Taking bind-over under advisement to allow briefing is not a departure from the rules — and in *Robinson* the briefing schedule was **defense-requested and mutually agreed**, with both sides wanting the hearing transcript before arguing probable cause ([East Idaho News](https://www.eastidahonews.com/2026/07/tyler-robinson-preliminary-hearing-ends-for-now-will-resume-in-september/)).

**Two contemporaneous Utah capital cases did exactly the same thing** — including one before a different judge in a different district, and one from the *same* prosecutor's office:

**What the pattern shows.** Same-day bench rulings happen — but in Utah they cluster in the **shorter (1–2 day), non-capital or death-withdrawn** prelims (*Richins*, *Baum*). The **live-capital, heavily contested** hearings (*Bate*, *Jayne*, *Robinson*) are the ones taken under advisement with post-hearing briefing and a multi-month delay. So **the delay tracks the seriousness of the case, not the identity of the judge.** And Robinson's timeline is actually **on the faster end**: his bind-over is argued **Sept 1, 2026 — about 8 weeks after** the July 10 hearing; *Jayne*'s bind-over argument came **~12 weeks after** its March 26 hearing close; and *Bate*'s bind-over decision is set for **Dec. 18, 2026 — roughly six months after** its June prelim. Tellingly, even the **victim's family is pushing *for* the Sept-1 timeline, not against it** — Erika Kirk and Charlie's parents filed a [[2026-07-11 - Victim Family Statement for Speedy Bindover|statement urging a ruling "no later than September 1, 2026"]] under the victim's speedy-disposition right — so the pace is a **case-wide feature both sides and the victims accept**, not one judge dragging his feet.

**What *is* unusual about *Robinson* is the case, not the timeline:** a **5-day** preliminary hearing (longer than the 1–2 day capital prelims above), the volume and complexity of the evidence, the capital stakes, and extraordinary publicity. The "extensive prelim + briefing + ~2-month delay to a bind-over decision" sequence itself is ordinary. See [[Judge Tony Graf]].

## 2. How long do Utah capital cases take?
**Charging to trial runs years, not months** — and appeals run decades.

- **Jerrod Baum:** charged April 2018 → trial ~March 2022 (**~4 years**).
- **Kouri Richins:** arrested May 2023 → trial Feb 2026 (**~2.75 years**) — and that case is **not** capital and had **no** contested pretrial appeals.
- Against that backdrop, Robinson's ~10-month charge-to-preliminary-hearing interval is **fast**, and a ~2-month gap between the hearing and a bind-over ruling is minor.

**And a death sentence, if ever imposed, is measured in decades.** Nationally, prisoners **executed in 2023** had spent an average of **279 months — 23.25 years — from sentence to execution** ([BJS, *Capital Punishment, 2023*](https://bjs.ojp.gov/document/cp23st.pdf), Table 7, July 2025). Utah's own history says the same (next section).

## 3. Utah's actual death-penalty record
Utah authorizes capital punishment but **rarely carries it out.** Since the modern era began (post-*Gregg v. Georgia*, 1976), **Utah juries have imposed 26 death sentences — none since 2008 — and the state has carried out just 8 executions**, with **0 clemencies and 0 exonerations** ([DPIC, "Spotlight on Utah's Death Penalty," Sept 2025](https://deathpenaltyinfo.org/spotlight-on-utahs-death-penalty)).

**Every Utah execution since 1976** (3 by firing squad, 5 by lethal injection):

| # | Name | Executed | Crime (yr) | Method | Conviction → execution |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Gary Gilmore | Jan 17, 1977 | 1976 | Firing squad | **3 months** *(he demanded it; waived appeals)* |
| 2 | Pierre Dale Selby | Aug 28, 1987 | 1974 (Ogden Hi-Fi murders) | Lethal injection | 12 yr 9 mo |
| 3 | Arthur Gary Bishop | Jun 10, 1988 | 1979–83 | Lethal injection | 4 yr 3 mo |
| 4 | William Andrews | Jul 30, 1992 | 1974 (Hi-Fi murders) | Lethal injection | 17 yr 8 mo |
| 5 | John Albert Taylor | Jan 26, 1996 | 1989 | Firing squad | 6 yr 1 mo |
| 6 | Joseph Mitchell Parsons | Oct 15, 1999 | 1987 | Lethal injection | 12 yr 1 mo |
| 7 | Ronnie Lee Gardner | Jun 18, 2010 | 1985 | Firing squad | 24 yr 8 mo |
| 8 | Taberon Dave Honie | Aug 8, 2024 | 1998 | Lethal injection | ~25 yr |

*Sources: [Utah Legislature, "A History of the Death Penalty in Utah"](https://le.utah.gov/interim/2016/pdf/00002802.pdf) (conviction/execution dates); [DPIC Utah](https://deathpenaltyinfo.org/state-and-federal-info/state-by-state/utah); [KUER on Honie](https://www.kuer.org/politics-government/2024-08-08/utah-executes-death-row-inmate-taberon-honie-by-lethal-injection).*

**Gilmore is the outlier that proves the rule.** His 3-month timeline (Jan 1977) happened only because he **fired his lawyers and demanded execution**, waiving appeals. **Every contested Utah case since has taken 4 to 25 years** — and the two most recent (Gardner ~25 yrs, Honie ~26 yrs from the crime) took a quarter-century.

**Honie (Aug 2024)** was Utah's **first execution in ~14 years** (since Gardner, June 2010) — for a **1998** murder ([CBS](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/utah-carries-out-its-first-execution-since-2010-taberon-honie-killed-his-girlfriends-mother-by-slitting-her-throat/)).

**Utah's death row now holds just 3 people** ([Wikipedia, "Capital punishment in Utah," Nov 2025](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_punishment_in_Utah)): **Michael Archuleta** (sentenced 1989), **Von Lester Taylor** (1991), and **Troy Kell** (1996). It shrank from ~7 in 2024 because Honie was executed, **Douglas Lovell**'s death sentence was **overturned** (2024), **Douglas Carter** won a **new trial** (affirmed by the Utah Supreme Court May 2025), and —

**Ralph Menzies died of natural causes on Nov 26, 2025**, age 67, after ~37 years on death row. His September 2025 **firing-squad** execution had been **stayed by the Utah Supreme Court** over **vascular dementia** — an evaluator found he could no longer understand why he was being executed — and he **died before the competency fight was resolved** ([Utah DOC](https://corrections.utah.gov/inmate-ralph-l-menzies-passes-away/); [Salt Lake Tribune](https://www.sltrib.com/news/2025/11/26/utah-death-row-inmate-ralph/); [CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2025/11/27/us/menzies-utah-dementia-inmate-dies)). Menzies is the **fourth** person to die of natural causes on Utah's modern death row (with Roberto Arguelles, Floyd Maestas, and Ron Lafferty). His death prompted a **2026 bill (HB495)** to speed up capital appeals — aiming to cut Utah's ~34-year average toward ~20 years ([Utah News Dispatch, Mar 2026](https://utahnewsdispatch.com/2026/03/04/utah-bill-to-speed-up-timeline-on-death-penalty-moves-forward/)).

> **The firing squad is still on Utah's books.** A 2015 law restored it as the **backup method** if the state cannot obtain lethal-injection drugs within 30 days of a scheduled execution; lethal injection is primary ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_punishment_in_Utah)). Menzies was slated for the firing squad under that law.

## 4. What a death sentence actually means (national data)
Even where the death penalty is *imposed*, it is **usually not carried out.** Of the **~9,000+ death sentences** handed down nationally since 1972, only about **1 in 6 to 1 in 5 (≈16–18%) have ended in execution** — while the single **most likely** outcome (~41%) is that the conviction or sentence is **reversed or the person is resentenced** ([DPIC, "Epic Fail" census analysis, 2026](https://deathpenaltyinfo.org/epic-fail-researchers-find-systemic-problems-persist-with-fewer-than-1-in-5-death-sentences-ending-in-execution)).

| What happens to a death sentence (1972–2025, ~9,000+) | Approx. share |
|---|---|
| **Executed** | **~16–18%** (1 in 6 to 1 in 5) |
| **Reversed / vacated / resentenced** | **~41%** (the most common outcome) |
| Died on death row (natural/suicide) | ~9% |
| Commuted / clemency | ~4% |
| Still on death row | ~22% |
| **Exonerated** (subset of reversals) | **202 people** since 1973 |

*Source: [DPIC "Epic Fail" / Death Penalty Census](https://deathpenaltyinfo.org/facts-and-research/data/death-penalty-census) and [DPIC Fact Sheet, July 2, 2026](https://files.deathpenaltyinfo.org/documents/pdf/FactSheet.pdf). Categories overlap and are rounded.*

Other anchors (all national):
- **1,670** total executions since 1976 ([DPIC](https://deathpenaltyinfo.org/executions/executions-overview), July 2, 2026).
- **Average sentence→execution: 23.25 years** (2023 cohort, [BJS](https://bjs.ojp.gov/document/cp23st.pdf)); death-row prisoners already average ~22 years served.
- **New death sentences are at historic lows** — 23 nationally in 2025, down from a peak of 325 in 1986 (DPIC Fact Sheet).
- **A sentence imposed in the mid-2020s that is *ever* carried out would, on current trends, most likely be executed in the mid-2040s or later** — if it survives the appellate reversal rate at all.

## 5. Utah vs. the high-volume states (the Florida contrast)
The death penalty is concentrated in a few states, and **Utah is among the lowest-volume.** Utah has carried out **~2 executions in the last 16 years** (Gardner 2010, Honie 2024). By contrast:

- **Florida executed 19 people in 2025** — a modern **state record**, ~40% of the national total, roughly one every 16 days ([DPIC](https://deathpenaltyinfo.org/new-report-examines-floridas-unprecedented-execution-pace-and-trends-in-2025)) — and **9 more by late June 2026**.
- The **most recent U.S. execution** (as of this writing) was **Dusty Ray Spencer**, Florida, **June 25, 2026** ([DPIC 2026 list](https://deathpenaltyinfo.org/executions/2026)).
- **Nationally: 47 executions in 2025** — a 16-year high — and 16 through late June 2026 ([DPIC Fact Sheet](https://files.deathpenaltyinfo.org/documents/pdf/FactSheet.pdf)).

So a death sentence's real-world meaning depends heavily on the state. Utah's record — 8 executions in ~50 years, none of a contested case in under 4 years, a death row that shrinks by reversal and natural death faster than by execution — is the opposite of Florida's current pace.

## 6. Would a case like this plead out?
You asked whether cases with seemingly overwhelming evidence plead out after the preliminary hearing. The honest answer: **guilty pleas dominate the system, but capital cases are the exception where trials remain common** — and it takes **two sides** to make a plea.

- **Pleas dominate generally.** 97% of federal and 94% of state convictions come from guilty pleas (*Missouri v. Frye*, 566 U.S. 134 (2012), [Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/10-444)).
- **Capital-specific data is thin,** but the one major empirical study (Georgia, 1993–2000) found **59% of capitally-charged cases pled and 41% went to trial**, and that a death notice raises the chance of a plea by ~20–25 points ([Thaxton, *Leveraging Death*](https://scholarlycommons.law.northwestern.edu/jclc/vol103/iss2/3/)). There is **no reliable national "capital plea rate."**
- **The mechanism** is that the prosecution can **withdraw the death notice in exchange for a guilty plea to life without parole (LWOP)**. That only happens if the State is willing to deal.

Real examples cut both ways:

| Defendant | Facing death, evidence strong? | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Joseph DeAngelo** (Golden State Killer) | Yes | **Pled guilty 2020**, death dropped → 11 life terms ([DPIC](https://deathpenaltyinfo.org/74-year-old-golden-state-killer-joseph-deangelo-pleads-guilty-to-13-murders-and-rapes-gets-11-life-sentences)) |
| **Jared Loughner** (Tucson/Giffords) | Yes | **Pled guilty 2012**, death barred → 7 life terms ([DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/jared-lee-loughner-pleads-guilty-federal-charges-tucson-shooting)) |
| **Nikolas Cruz** (Parkland) | Yes | Pled guilty to guilt, but **State pursued death anyway**; jury chose **LWOP** (2022) ([DPIC](https://deathpenaltyinfo.org/non-unanimous-florida-jury-sentences-nikolas-cruz-to-life-without-parole-for-parkland-school-shootings)) |
| **Robert Bowers** (Pittsburgh synagogue) | Yes | Offered LWOP plea; **DOJ refused, went to trial → death** (2023) ([DPIC](https://deathpenaltyinfo.org/jurors-sentence-robert-bowers-to-death-for-2018-synagogue-shooting)) |

**Why a strong-evidence capital defendant may still go to trial:** when the State won't take death off the table, the defendant facing likely death has comparatively little to lose by contesting guilt and, above all, by fighting at the **bifurcated penalty phase**, where mitigation (mental illness, trauma, brain damage) can persuade even a single juror to reject death — as in *Cruz*. This is why the [[The Established Record|strength of the evidence on guilt]] does not, by itself, predict a plea in a capital case. See [[Confessions and Admissions]], [[Beating Some Charges Losing Others]].

## 7. National comparisons — how Robinson's *process* and *pace* stack up
The assassination/murder cases people reach for as comparisons actually **underscore how transparent and normal-paced Robinson's case is.**

**How each was charged — and why Robinson's is the *open* path.** Serious cases reach trial two ways: a **grand jury** (secret, prosecutor-only, no defense, no cross) hands up an **indictment**, or a **magistrate** binds the case over after a **public preliminary hearing** where the defense can cross-examine.

| | **Grand jury indictment** | **Preliminary hearing** *(Robinson)* |
|---|---|---|
| Setting | **Secret**, closed | **Public**, open court |
| Who's present | Prosecutor + grand jurors | Prosecutor, **defendant + defense counsel**, judge |
| Adversarial? | **No** — no defense, no cross | **Yes** — defense cross-examines |
| Standard | Probable cause | Probable cause |
| Transparency | Low (testimony sealed) | High (evidence aired live) |

- **Luigi Mangione** (UnitedHealthcare CEO killing, Dec 2024): charged the **secret grand-jury way** — a NY state indictment ([Dec 17, 2024](https://manhattanda.org/d-a-bragg-announces-murder-indictment-of-luigi-mangione/)) and a **federal indictment** ([Apr 17, 2025](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/luigi-mangione-charged-stalking-and-murder-unitedhealthcare-ceo-brian-thompson-and-use)).
- **Derek Chauvin** (George Floyd, 2020): state case by **complaint** (Minnesota permits it); federal case by **grand jury** ([trial record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trial_of_Derek_Chauvin)).
- **James Earl Ray** (MLK, 1968): **grand-jury indicted**, then **pled guilty — no trial at all** ([HSCA / National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/research/jfk/select-committee-report/part-2a.html)).
- **Robinson** proceeds by **prosecutor's Information + a public preliminary hearing** — the adversarial, in-open-court probable-cause path. That is why his process looks unusually transparent: the State's witnesses and exhibits were **tested live and on the record over five days**, not behind a grand jury's closed doors.

**How long these take — Robinson is *not* slow.**

| Case | Charged how | Crime → resolution | Elapsed |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ray** (MLK) | Grand jury → **plea** | Apr 1968 → Mar 1969 | **~11 mo** (guilty plea, no trial) |
| **Chauvin** (state) | Complaint | May 2020 → Apr 2021 verdict | **~11 mo** to verdict |
| **Mangione** | Grand jury (state) + federal | Dec 2024 → **unresolved** | **~19 mo, ongoing** |
| **Robinson** | Information + public prelim | Sept 2025 → **pre-bindover** | **~10 mo, ongoing** |

The two that *resolved* (Ray by plea, Chauvin by verdict) each closed in **~11 months** — but both moved fast for specific reasons (Ray pled; Chauvin's proof was a single continuous video). **Mangione — the closest analog to Robinson** (recent, high-profile, hotly contested, no plea) — is **~19 months in and still unresolved**, with its **federal death-eligible counts struck** (Jan 2026) and NY **terrorism counts dismissed** (Sept 2025). Against that, Robinson's ~10-month, pre-bindover posture is **squarely normal.**

**"Jurors lie or forget" — and a trial can still stand.** A recurring worry (sharpened by the [[Prosecution Problems|64%-believe-guilty]] venue survey) is that no fair jury is possible in a saturated media environment. **Chauvin** is the instructive precedent: after the verdict, juror **Brandon Mitchell** turned out to have attended the **Aug. 2020 "Get Your Knee Off Our Necks" march** wearing a **BLM shirt bearing that exact slogan** — which he had **not disclosed** on his jury questionnaire. The defense moved for a **new trial and a juror-misconduct (*Schwartz*) hearing**; Judge Cahill **denied both** (June 25, 2021), and the **conviction was affirmed on appeal** ([Courthouse News](https://www.courthousenews.com/chauvin-asks-for-new-trial-over-death-of-george-floyd/), [GBH](https://www.wgbh.org/news/national/2021-06-25/judge-rejects-chauvin-request-for-new-trial-in-floyd-death)). The lesson cuts against the "impossible to seat a fair jury" claim: even in the **most saturated venue imaginable**, with a juror who held and *marched on* strong views, the trial stood. **Voir dire, not a venue change, is the usual answer** — a point that also tempers the defense's own publicity argument.

**The MLK autopsy parallel.** Like Kirk, Dr. King was killed by a **single .30-06 soft-point** round, and — like Kirk's reported wound — the fatal bullet was **recovered from the body**, having traveled a **downward path through the neck/spine** (the HSCA/Baden panel traced it: entry below the mouth, **fracturing the jaw, exiting the lower face and re-entering the neck**, severing arteries and fracturing the spine, coming to rest in the back). It is a **retained-projectile** case — though *not* a clean "no exit," and its **jaw-first deceleration** is a mechanism Kirk's neck wound lacks. Full treatment: [[The Missing Exit Wound]], [[The Medical Examiner and the Wound]].

## Bottom line for the Robinson case
1. **Graf's pace is normal.** Post-hearing briefing and a multi-month delay to a capital bind-over decision is standard Utah practice right now (*Bate*, *Jayne*) — the delay reflects the seriousness of the case, not an idiosyncratic judge. The unusual features are the **5-day hearing and the publicity.**
2. **Everything from here runs in years.** Utah capital cases take years to reach trial; a death sentence, if ever imposed and upheld, averages **~23 years** to an execution, and Utah has not carried one out in under 4 years of contested litigation in the modern era.
3. **A death *sentence* is not a death.** Nationally only ~16–18% of death sentences end in execution; the most common outcome is reversal. Utah's row shrinks more by reversal and natural death than by execution — its **most recent** death-row exit was **Menzies dying of dementia**, not an execution.
4. **A plea is possible but far from certain.** It would require the State to withdraw the death notice; even overwhelming guilt evidence often does not stop a capital case from going to a penalty-phase trial.
