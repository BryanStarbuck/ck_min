         1.      Gannett Co. v. DePasquale

         First, Defendant cites the 1979 case of Gannett Co. v. DePasquale, 443 U.S. 368 (1979),

and asserts (at 3) that it “is more analogous to the circumstances here” than the Supreme Court’s

subsequent and extensive authority on the First Amendment right of access. That assertion is

puzzling, to say the least. Gannett was not a First Amendment access case. It dealt instead with

the Public Trial clause of the Sixth Amendment, which guarantees a defendant the right to a

public trial. Id. at 379. The issue was whether the news media or the public, as opposed to the

defendant, could invoke the Public Trial clause to obtain access to closed hearings. Id. at 382-

83. In a fractured plurality opinion, the Court held that the Public Trial clause of the Sixth

Amendment is for the benefit of the defendant, and the public does not have any independent

right to assert it to obtain access. Id.; see also Ark. Television Co. v. Tedder, 662 S.W.2d 174,

175 (Ark. 1983) (“The only clear holding agreed on by a majority of the [Gannett] Court was a

finding that the sixth amendment did not provide the public with a constitutional right of access

to pretrial suppression hearings.” (emphasis added)).

         As for the First Amendment, contrary to Defendant’s implication, the Court did not even

reach that question, noting old precedent holding there was no such right, but stating “we do not

decide” whether the First Amendment provides a right of access, much less what such a right

would look like or the test to overcome it. Gannett, 443 U.S. at 392.

         Which makes sense, because Gannett was decided in 1979, a year before the Court first

expressly recognized the public’s First Amendment right of access. In the term immediately

         2.      State v. Archuleta

         Second, Defendant cites State v. Archuleta, 857 P.2d 234 (Utah 1993), and asserts (at 4) it

is “[c]onsistent with” Gannett and “instructive here.” But because Archuleta actually did

concern the public’s right of access under the First Amendment and the Utah Constitution, it has

nothing to do with the Sixth Amendment issue in Gannett. And Defendant’s invocation of that

case is equally puzzling given its full-throated recognition of the public’s constitutional right of

access to pretrial documents filed in court proceedings. Id. at 238-39. Defendant is correct that

the Archuleta court affirmed the district court’s denial of access to certain specific evidentiary

documents, albeit on different grounds, and because the privilege is, of course, qualified. But

Defendant does not say anything about why that conclusion was reached in that case on its

unique and unusual facts, nor does he attempt to equate them to the subject matter of

Defendant’s motion here. The records restricted in Archuleta contained “gruesome” and

“horrific” details about a “homicide [that] was committed in a particularly depraved manner,

consisting of physical torture, anal object rape, and massive bodily injury to the victim prior to

death,” as well as “information concerning sodomy, physical torture to the victim’s testicles with

battery cables, and anal penetration with a tire iron.” Id. at 236, 239 & n.21.

         Because the defense has refused to provide the News Media with their underlying

motion, the News Media (and the public) are left to guess what the supposed evidence is that

Defendant seeks to exclude. But given the circumstances of the alleged crime, they are certain it

is not that, or likely even in the same ballpark.

           To put a sharper point on it, no one has ever argued the public’s constitutional right of

access is absolute and can never, even in rare or unusual circumstances, be overcome. It is a

qualified right. But Defendant’s attempt to spin Archuleta and Gannett into a categorical

argument that the public has no right of access to any pretrial suppression motions or the

evidence they concern, no matter their content, has no grounding in any constitutional precedent.

         3.      United States v. McVeigh

         Third, Defendant cites United States v. McVeigh, 119 F.3d 806 (10th Cir. 1997) (per

curiam), for the assertion (at 5) that the public has no right “to inadmissible evidence.” But

Defendant again does not explain what was actually at issue in McVeigh. That case does not

support Defendant’s argument. 2

         As a threshold matter, the question in McVeigh was not primarily access to pretrial

suppression hearings or associated motion papers, as Defendant incorrectly implies. The

question, instead, was whether the public had a right to access underlying evidence that was

excluded (and discussed in redacted portions of the publicly filed papers) after an open

suppression hearing had been held in the full light of day. Indeed, in the same breath, the Tenth

Circuit explicitly noted that were it to need to decide the issue, the Press-Enterprise II test would

be satisfied for suppression motions:

         Also, unlike McVeigh, there has been no ruling in this case that the evidence at issue is

inadmissible. In McVeigh, the court was reviewing on appeal, under an abuse of discretion

standard, the release of certain information where there had already been a determination that the

evidence was inadmissible. That posture is entirely unlike this case, where Defendant is merely

asserting that certain evidence should be excluded. No court has held that the mere possibility

that evidence will be excluded at the trial court level is sufficient to prophylactically seal all

evidentiary motions until a ruling is made. To reach that conclusion would turn the presumption

of access on its head and permit every single motion to exclude evidence to be filed under seal,

contrary to the very right of access noted in McVeigh. See, e.g., New York v. Arroyo, 675

N.Y.S.2d 272 (N.Y. Cty. Ct. 1998) (distinguishing McVeigh in part because “the redacted items

pertaining to the suppression issue had been ruled inadmissible”).

         In addition, and perhaps most fundamental, the McVeigh court allowed certain evidence

to be redacted based upon a finding that “[a]ccess to the redacted information is not needed for a

full understanding of the court’s decision on the motion to suppress[.]” McVeigh, 119 F.3d at

813. That was not only because “the suppression hearing itself was open,” id., but also because

reasons for suppression in that case did not depend on the actual content of the suppressed

evidence. Id. at 813. Rather, the court’s suppression order was based on the circumstances

under which the evidence was obtained—whether the defendant’s interview with the FBI was

unlawful, and whether the notes taken in that interview were hearsay. Id. at 809, 814 (“Thus,

both the press and the public had ample opportunity to understand the circumstances

surrounding Nichols’ statements, and the reasons why those statements were deemed

         In this case, although the News Media has unjustifiably been prevented from seeing the

actual arguments Defendant is making, it is quite likely those arguments are based on the content

of the evidence at issue. Denying the public access to that information will necessarily preclude

the public from understanding the nature of the proceedings and the basis for any ruling by the

court. Unlike McVeigh, there is no way for the public to have a “full understanding of the

court’s decision on the motion to suppress” without knowing what the evidence at issue is. Id. at

813. This case is much more like other cases where content-based evidentiary motions have not

justified closure, and the public has been granted access to evidence despite its inadmissibility. 3

         In any event, the McVeigh court’s language regarding underlying, inadmissible evidence

on appeal provides no authority for keeping motion papers merely asserting inadmissibility

under seal at the district court level. Defendant has voluntarily brought an exclusion motion

seeking a public court ruling presumably based on the content of the evidence at issue. Having

done so, he cannot complain that the content of the evidence is now subject to the public’s

constitutional right of access.
