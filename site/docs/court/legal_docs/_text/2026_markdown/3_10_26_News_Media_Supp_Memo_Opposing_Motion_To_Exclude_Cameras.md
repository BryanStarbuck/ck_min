                                       INTRODUCTION

            The 181-page Motion to Exclude devotes pages upon pages to critiquing “the

media,” ruminating on statements by public officials, and surmising that various officials and

commentators violated ethical rules and even the law. Those topics are a distraction from the

narrow issue before the Court: whether the defense has made a non-speculative, specific showing

that is sufficiently compelling to overcome the presumption favoring EMC. It has not.

            A fundamental flaw in the defense’s argument is that it recounts the publicity

surrounding this case before leaping to the conclusion that EMC must be terminated. But there

has been no showing of prejudice, let alone prejudice attributable to EMC. Indeed, much of the

publicity the defense complains of—including statements by the Governor and the President—

exist independently of EMC and could not be addressed by terminating EMC.



                                                 1
            The defense also refuses to recognize the benefits of EMC. As has been briefed at

length, the public has a right to access criminal proceedings. This right “promote[s] an informed

discussion of government affairs” and “ensure[s] the fairness of the criminal trial.” Kearns-

Tribune Corp. v. Lewis, 685 P.2d 515, 518 (Utah 1984). EMC helps members of the public

assert this right by ensuring that all individuals can access the court proceedings. Certainly,

there will be those who sensationalize and share inaccurate information, but terminating EMC

will not solve that problem. On the other hand, EMC allows members of the public to see and

hear the proceedings, giving them the most accurate information and the ability to discern for

themselves whether other sources are trustworthy.

            Finally, the defense disregards the traditional methods for ensuring a fair and

impartial jury, asserting this case is unique given the widespread publicity. But Utah has had

high-profile cases before, and in those cases, courts recognized the efficacy of the traditional

methods. Targeted measures like searching voir dire are the most effective for preventing

prejudice—not blanket bans on EMC that attempt to control the public discourse.

            At bottom, the defense asks this Court to conclude that there cannot be a fair trial if

EMC continues. The 181-page Motion covers many topics, but it does not come close to making

a showing that can support that conclusion.

I.     The News Media Has Standing to Oppose the Motion to Exclude.

       The Motion to Exclude does not argue that the News Media lacks standing. But to lay

any doubts to rest, the News Media has standing.

       Standing requires (1) an injury, (2) a causal relationship between the injury and the

challenged actions, and (3) a request for relief that is likely to redress the injury. Laws v.

Grayeyes, 2021 UT 59, ¶ 27, 498 P.3d 410. To show injury, “a party must demonstrate a

‘particularized injury’ ‘that gives [it] a personal stake in the outcome of the legal dispute.’” Id.

¶ 28 (quoting Jenkins v. Swan, 675 P.2d 1145, 1148, 1151 (Utah 1983)). The injury must be

“distinct and palpable,” but it need not be a constitutional injury. Jenkins, 675 P.2d at 1148. The

injury analysis ensures that courts are not “drawn into disputes that are not fit for judicial

resolution or amount to generalized grievances that are more appropriately directed to the

legislative and executive branches of the state government.” Id. (cleaned up).

       The News Media has a personal stake in the outcome of this dispute. Rule 4-401.01

creates a presumption favoring EMC “by a news reporter … where the predominant purpose of

the electronic media coverage request is journalism or dissemination of news to the public.”

Utah Code Jud. Admin. R. 4-401.01(2). Should the Motion to Exclude be granted, the News

Media would experience a distinct and palpable injury not shared with the general public—

denial of EMC coverage. See Soc’y of Pro. Journalists v. Bullock, 743 P.2d 1166, 1174 (Utah

1987) (“[T]he requirement of adverse impact is met by anyone who claims a right of access to a

judicial proceeding and then actually takes steps to exercise that right but is denied access.”).

And because the Court is in the best position to rule on the Motion to Exclude, the News Media’s

grievance is not one that is better addressed by the legislative or executive branches. Id. (“The

issue—whether a constitutional right of access exists and is being denied—is of a type that is

                                                  3
committed to the judiciary for resolution. Indeed, it is hard to imagine how the issue of access,

especially access to judicial proceedings, could be resolved by any other branch.”).

        The remaining requirements, causation and redressability, are also easily satisfied. See

id. (“In the context of challenges to court closures by those actually attempting to exercise access

rights, [the second and third] requirements are easily met.”). There is “a direct causal connection

between” the defense’s request in the Motion to Exclude and the injury that will be suffered—the

denial of EMC. Id. And the relief sought by the News Media—denying the Motion to

Exclude—“would provide a direct remedy for the anticipated injury.” Id.

        For these reasons, the News Media has standing to oppose the Motion to Exclude. That

conclusion is not novel and has been the position of Utah courts for decades. 1

        Separately, the News Media also has public interest standing. “Utah law … allows

parties to gain standing if they can show that they are an appropriate party raising issues of

significant public interest.” Haik v. Jones, 2018 UT 39, ¶ 23, 427 P.3d 1155 (omission in

original) (quoting Gregory v. Shurtleff, 2013 UT 18, ¶ 18, 299 P.3d 1098). A party has public

interest standing if the issue is of “significant public interest” and the party invoking standing is

an “appropriate party.” Id. (quoting Gregory, 2013 UT 18, ¶¶ 27–28). Those elements are easily

satisfied here.

        First, whether EMC will be terminated for the remainder of these proceedings is of

significant public interest. That interest is demonstrated by Utah’s presumption favoring EMC,

II.      EMC Is Consistent with the Constitutional Interests at Stake.

         The defense spills much ink downplaying and attempting to discredit the presumption

favoring EMC. But the presumption is the law, and it complements important constitutional

rights. And although the defense relies on a “per se rule” against EMC in “notorious cases,”

there is no such rule.

         A.      EMC serves important constitutional interests.

         As has been briefed at length, there is a constitutional right of access to criminal

proceedings. 2 Richmond Newspapers, Inc. v. Virginia, 448 U.S. 555, 580 (1980) (“We hold that

the right to attend criminal trials is implicit in the guarantees of the First Amendment; without

the freedom to attend such trials, which people have exercised for centuries, important aspects of

freedom of speech and of the press could be eviscerated.” (cleaned up)); Kearns-Tribune Corp.


2
    For a more comprehensive discussion of this right, see Dkt. 220 at 5–8.


                                                   5
v. Lewis, 685 P.2d 515, 518–21 (Utah 1984) (holding that under the U.S. and Utah Constitutions,

“people have a constitutional right of public access to criminal trials and preliminary hearings”).

       Although there is a public right of access, it is obviously impossible for most of the

public to attend proceedings. Courtrooms have limited capacities, and regardless, most citizens

are unable to attend for various reasons, such as the timing and location of proceedings. These

limitations present a serious threat to public access—“what exists of the right of access if it

extends only to those who can squeeze through the door?” United States v. Antar, 38 F.3d 1348,

1360 (3d Cir. 1994).

       Given these constraints, the press plays an important role in asserting the right to public

access. “Instead of acquiring information about trials by firsthand observation or by word of

mouth from those who attend, people now acquire it chiefly through the print and electronic

media.” Richmond Newspapers, 448 U.S. at 573–74. The press thus functions as “surrogates in

asserting the public’s constitutional right of access.” Kearns-Tribune, 685 P.2d at 522. This

“result[s] in far more general dissemination of information about judicial proceedings than the

attendance of any other members of the public.” Id.; see also Richmond Newspapers, 448 U.S.

at 573 (recognizing that the press functions “as surrogates for the public,” which “contributes to

public understanding of the rule of law and comprehension of the functioning of the entire

criminal justice system” (cleaned up)).

       Just as news reporting ensures that the public can access court proceedings, so too does

EMC. In fact, EMC increases the accuracy of reporting by allowing members of the public to

see and hear exactly what transpires and to form their own judgments. See Soc’y of Pro.

Journalists v. Sec’y of Labor, 616 F. Supp. 569, 578 (D. Utah 1985) (“[T]he full flavor of the

hearing cannot be sensed from the sterile sheets of a transcript. Emotions, gestures, facial



                                                  6
expressions, and pregnant pauses do not appear on the reported transcript.”); ABC, Inc. v.

Stewart, 360 F.3d 90, 99 (2d Cir. 2004) (“[O]ne cannot describe an anguished look or a nervous

tic.”). So EMC works in tandem with the public’s constitutional right of access by ensuring that

all members of the public can access the courtroom and the most accurate information.

       EMC also complements the defendant’s fair trial rights. The News Media has never

argued, as the defense suggests, that the public’s right to access trumps the defendant’s fair trial

rights. Instead, the News Media has always maintained that EMC coexists with and even

augments fair trial rights. Press-Enterprise Co. v. Sup. Ct. of Cal. for Riverside Cnty., 478 U.S.

1, 7 (1986) (“Plainly, the defendant has a right to a fair trial but, as we have repeatedly

recognized, one of the important means of assuring a fair trial is that the process be open to

neutral observers.”); id. (“The right to an open public trial is a shared right of the accused and the

public, the common concern being the assurance of fairness.”); Kearns-Tribune, 685 P.2d at 518

(“Another reason cited for the First Amendment right of access is to ensure the fairness of the

criminal trial.”). That is not to say there are never situations where EMC can be restricted. But

the appropriate way to address those situations is on a hearing-by-hearing basis and with targeted

restrictions, not through blanket bans on all EMC in all future proceedings.

       B.      There is no “per se rule” against EMC in “notorious” cases.

       The defense argues that the U.S. Supreme Court created a “per se rule” against EMC in

“notorious cases.” (Motion to Exclude at 58, 78–79, 81; Reply in Support of Motion to Exclude,

Dkt. 399 at 8.) The defense purports to find its “rule” in a clumsy blend of three U.S. Supreme

Court cases: Estes v. Texas, 381 U.S. 532 (1965); Chandler v. Florida, 449 U.S. 560 (1981); and

Hollingsworth v. Perry, 558 U.S. 183 (2010) (per curiam). None of these cases—considered

together or separate—create any “per se” rules about EMC. In fact, just the opposite.



                                                  7
         Estes v. Texas. In Estes, the Court reversed a conviction based on the “considerable

disruption” caused by obsolete technology and a dozen cameramen:

         [A]t least 12 cameramen were engaged in the courtroom throughout the hearing
         taking motion and still pictures and televising the proceedings. Cables and wires
         were snaked across the courtroom floor, three microphones were on the judge’s
         bench and others were beamed at the jury box and the counsel table. It is conceded
         that the activities of the television crews and news photographers led to
         considerable disruption of the hearings.

381 U.S. at 536; see also id. at 550 (“In addition, the courtroom was a mass of wires, television

cameras, microphones and photographers.”).

         Those concerns, while perhaps legitimate at the time, are not present here—there is a

single pool photographer with modern, unobtrusive technology. Also, the district court in Estes

“announced no restrictions,” differing from this Court’s proactive approach. Id. at 551. And the

Estes Court explicitly recognized that its ruling was limited to the facts and circumstances before

it. Id. at 540 (“When the advances in these arts permit reporting by printing press or by

television without their present hazards to a fair trial we will have another case.”).

         The Estes Court at no point created any rules about “notorious cases.” Perhaps

recognizing this, the defense relies on a concurring opinion. (See Motion to Exclude at 81;

Reply in Support of Motion to Exclude at 8.) But of course, a concurrence does not bind any

court.

         Chandler v. Florida. In Chandler, the Court made clear that Estes did not announce “a

constitutional rule barring still photographic, radio, and television coverage.” 449 U.S. at 574.

And the Chandler Court refused to adopt such a rule. Id. at 574–81. The Court also declined to

reverse a conviction on the grounds that it was televised, concluding the defendant had failed to

show that the coverage denied him due process. Id. at 581. The Chandler Court did not

discuss—let alone adopt—a “per se rule” regarding “notorious” cases. And when discussing


                                                  8
state rules concerning EMC, the Court remarked that “the states must be free to experiment.” Id.

at 582.

          Hollingsworth v. Perry. In Hollingsworth, the Court stayed the broadcast of a federal

civil trial. The ruling was based on procedural grounds—“the courts below did not follow the

appropriate procedures set forth in federal law before changing their rules to allow such

broadcasting.” 558 U.S. at 184. The Court did not “express[] any view on whether such trials

should be broadcast.” Id.

          Hollingsworth makes no reference to “notorious” cases or “per se” rules. Nonetheless,

the defense argues that “Hollingsworth establishes that at least five members of the present Court

believe that Justice Harlan’s concurring opinion in Estes is speaking for ‘the Court.’” (Motion to

Exclude at 81.) That is incorrect for the simple reason that only three of the current justices were

in the Hollingsworth majority. But even if the entire current Court had signed on to

Hollingsworth, the defense would still be wrong. Hollingsworth cited Justice Harlan’s

concurrence once, but a court does not adopt an entire concurrence as binding law by citing it

once. See id. at 195. Moreover, Hollingsworth did not discuss or adopt any “per se” rules

regarding EMC—it simply held there had been a procedural error in how a court rule was

adopted, and it explicitly did not “express[] any view on whether such trials should be

broadcast.” Id. at 184.

          In sum, there is no “per se” rule concerning “notorious” cases. To the contrary, U.S.

Supreme Court authority establishes that there are no per se rules concerning EMC, that states




                                                  9
“must be free to experiment” with EMC, and that a party objecting to EMC must demonstrate

prejudice. 3 Id. at 574, 581.

III.   The Defense Has Not Overcome the Presumption Favoring EMC.

       The defense devotes numerous pages to opposing Rule 4-401.01 on policy grounds.

Those arguments are unpersuasive and irrelevant—the Judicial Council has long since adopted

the Rule, and this Court cannot disregard it. The defense’s remaining arguments are also

unpersuasive. The Rule plainly applies, and the defense has not carried its burden of showing

the presumption has been overcome.

       A.      EMC is presumptively favored.

the electronic media coverage is journalism or dissemination of news to the public.” Utah Code

of Jud. Admin. R. 4-401.01(2)(A). As the News Media has already demonstrated—and the

Court has already ruled—the News Media’s predominant purpose in providing EMC is

journalism and dissemination of news to the public. (See Motions for Limited Intervention,

Dkts. 164 & 197; Requests & Orders for Electronic Media Coverage of Proceedings, Dkts. 25–

27, 66–67, 131, 133, 166, 177, 181, 193–94, 215, 222, 260, 319, 333, 397.)

defense merely rehashes the publicity surrounding this case before jumping to the unearned

conclusion that EMC should be terminated.

         This case will be tried by a jury—not the public—and the Court has tools to ensure a fair

and impartial jury that “render[s] a verdict based on the evidence.” Id. Those tools include “use

of an enlarged venire, thorough and searching voir dire, and a detailed jury questionnaire.” State

v. Allgier, 2011 UT 47, ¶ 19, 258 P.3d 589. The defense brushes these tools off, contending the

Court must “prevent prejudice, not merely [cure it] after the fact.” (Reply in Support of Motion

to Exclude at 7.) But empaneling an impartial jury does prevent prejudice. And while the Court

cannot control what every member of the public sees and hears, it can implement measures to

ensure that the jury actually seated is fair and impartial. These targeted measures do far more to

protect fair trial rights than blanket bans on EMC that attempt to control online commentary.

       Second, the defense has not shown a reasonable likelihood that safety will be jeopardized.

The defense’s main contention is that because of the publicity surrounding this case, there is a

looming “danger of violence” that justifies terminating EMC. (See Motion to Exclude at 155.)

The defense has no concrete or particularized evidence of this alleged threat. Instead, the

defense relies on a linguistic analysis of statements by public officials and online commentators.

(Motion to Exclude at 149–50.) This analysis is tenuous, at best, and does not say anything

specific about EMC—these same arguments could be used to support an argument (albeit a weak

one) that the proceedings should be closed to the public.

       The defense also ignores the safety benefits of EMC. When members of the public can

“attend” the proceedings virtually, there is less likelihood of a rush to the courthouse to squeeze

through the doors and vie for seating. And, as the U.S. Supreme Court recognized, “the open

processes of justice serve an important prophylactic purpose, providing an outlet for community

concern, hostility, and emotion.” Richmond Newspapers, 448 U.S. at 571. Contrary to the

defense’s argument, limiting access is more likely to interrupt this “community catharsis.” Id.

       Finally, it should go without saying that the comparison to Lee Harvey Oswald is

misguided. The News Media does not have—and has not asked for—“uninhibited access” to

areas through which Robinson passes. (Motion to Exclude at 154.)

       Third, the defense has not shown a reasonable likelihood of unwarranted privacy

invasions. The defense asserts Robinson’s own privacy interests, but none of the cited authority

concerns a defendant’s privacy interests at trial. (See Motion to Exclude at 158–61.) That is

unsurprising—a criminal defendant has no cognizable privacy interest in the subject matter of his

prosecution. Concerns about Robinson’s parents and unidentified witnesses are also unavailing.

What happens in court is public and subject to reporting. Craig v. Harney, 331 U.S. 367, 374



                                                 14
(1947) (“What transpires in the court room is public property.”); Sheppard v. Maxwell, 384 U.S.

333, 362–63 (1966) (“Of course, there is nothing that proscribes the press from reporting events

that transpire in the courtroom.”). So the defense’s only argument is that EMC is more intrusive.

But if that argument were sufficient, any criminal defendant could overcome the presumption by

broadly asserting witness privacy interests. That is not how the presumption works.

       The defense’s generalized concerns about publicity and “the media” do not show a

reasonable likelihood that EMC will prejudice fair trial rights, jeopardize safety, or constitute an

unwarranted invasion of privacy. The defense has not overcome the presumption favoring EMC.

       C.      Any alleged decorum order violations do not warrant terminating EMC, as
               this Court has already ruled.

       The defense also argues that EMC should be terminated due to alleged decorum order

violations. But the court has already ruled on these alleged violations and determined they do

not warrant terminating EMC. So there is no reason to revisit the issue, particularly where the

Court has amended the decorum order and relocated the camera to address the defense’s

concerns. Nonetheless, a few points are worth addressing.

       First, the defense relies on a single article—not by the News Media—that purports to lip

read Robinson’s statements to his defense team. The defense has not put forth any evidence that

the recording was in fact “decipherable by a lip reader,” which the Court recognized is an

uncertain phrasing. But in any event, the Court has already addressed this issue by relocating the

camera and clarifying that recording cannot include “zoom in.” (Standing Decorum Order

Effective Feb. 24, 2026, Dkt. 414.) Thus, there is no reason to reconsider this issue.

       Second, the defense points out that shackles were shown during the December 29, 2025

hearing. The News Media does not dispute but this, but the defense’s comparison to a “perp

walk” is overdone. Because the camera was in the jury box next to the defense table, it

camera’s direct and immediate line of sight. The camera has since been repositioned, addressing

this concern.
