I.     Mr. Robinson’s Request for Additional Time to Allow Counsel to Render Effective
       Assistance of Counsel Is Not Unusual and the State’s Suggestion That the
       Preliminary Hearing Proceed Without This Allowance Is a Constructive Denial of
       Mr. Robinson’s Rights Under the Sixth and Eighth Amendments, and the Utah
       Constitution, Article I, Section 12.

       There is nothing unusual about Mr. Robinson’s request to continue the preliminary

hearing in order for counsel to receive and have the opportunity to review discovery the defense

believes in good faith is necessary to provide effective assistance of counsel to Mr. Robinson at

the preliminary hearing. Further, it is not unreasonable for this Court to allow Mr. Robinson

sufficient time to determine based on that review what evidence he intends to present at the

preliminary hearing, whether it be through lay witnesses, other law enforcement witnesses, the

forensic experts identified in the reports the State intends to admit, or by Mr. Robinson calling




                                                                                        Page 5 of 17
his own forensic experts to testify. 2 The point is that without the underlying data that Mr.

Robinson has diligently requested, a fact the State does not dispute, the defense has insufficient

information to make those critical strategic decisions. In so doing, the State is forcing a denial of

Mr. Robinson’s rights to the effective assistance of counsel. See U.S. Const. amend. VI; Utah

Const. art. I, § 12; Menzies v. Galetka, 2006 UT 81, ¶ 99-100, 150 P.3d 480 (an attorney

abdicating his duties results in a forfeiture of the proceeding itself; “a forfeiture due to counsel’s

deficient representation renders the proceedings inherently unreliable”).

       As stated in the lodestar for ineffective assistance of counsel claims (and therefore the

inverse, guiding trial counsel on the effective assistance of counsel), Strickland v. Washington,

“[t]he right to counsel plays a crucial role in the adversarial system embodied in the Sixth

Amendment, since access to counsel’s skill and knowledge is necessary to accord defendants the

‘ample opportunity to meet the case of the prosecution’ to which they are entitled.” 466 U.S.

668, 685 (1984) (quoting Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 269, 275 (1942)).

The right to counsel embodies much more than mere presence. See id. And the “[g]overnment

violates the right to effective assistance when it interferes in certain ways with the ability of

counsel to make independent decisions about how to conduct the defense.” Id. at 686.

II.    State v. Lopez Does Not Apply; the Factors in Layton City v. Longcrier Provide
       Meaningful Guidance to the Court on the Issue of the Continuance.

that expert reports may be imbued with some presumption of reliability, but only if a defendant has

been provided with the discovery upon which those reports are based. See Mtn to Continue, at 9–

cases emanate from the Eighth Amendment’s Cruel and Unusual Punishment Clause but also flow

from the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. This constitutionally required “heightened

reliability” standard is not reserved only for penalty phase proceedings. As the Court explained in

IV.     The State’s Interpretation of Its Constitutional and Rule-Based Discovery
        Obligations Is Incorrect; the State Is Responsible for Seeking Out and Providing
        What Is Known and Requested.
        The State’s argument regarding its obligations under Utah Rule of Criminal Procedure

16, the governing rule anticipated by article I, section 12 of the Utah Constitution, is an effort to

relieve it of its burden to produce evidence to which Mr. Robinson is constitutionally entitled, is

known to the prosecution, and that has been “presently and reasonably available” to the

prosecution either before charges were filed against Mr. Robinson in September of 2025, or very

shortly thereafter. The State’s efforts to relieve itself of its constitutional obligation to provide

the requested discovery create an untenable loophole in Rule 16 and Utah Constitution, article I,

section 12 that results in no constitutional protections at all. See Opp. at 10–12.

        The State makes much of the phrase in Rule 16(a)(2), “to the prosecutor” in the

following:

         In every case, all material or information listed under paragraph (a)(1) that is
         presently and reasonably available to the prosecutor must be disclosed before
         the preliminary examination, if applicable, or before the defendant enters a
         plea of guilty or no contest or goes to trial, unless otherwise waived by the
         defendant.

       The prosecution interprets this to mean that the only evidence it need produce to the

defense prior to the preliminary hearing is that which its lawyers physically have in their

possession. However, this argument ignores the plain language incorporating the (a)(1)

mandatory disclosures and undermines the State’s constitutional obligation to go beyond its own

files to those of the entire prosecution team as part of its ongoing discovery obligations. See, e.g.,

Kyles v. Whitley, 514 U.S. 419, 437 (1995) (“the individual prosecutor has a duty to learn of any

favorable evidence known to the others acting on the government’s behalf in the case, including

the police.”). As a policy matter, such an interpretation would encourage or at least excuse bad

faith decisions by the State to do the opposite—to intentionally avoid seeking information from

its law enforcement partners prior to the preliminary hearing so it does not have to disclose it.

Mr. Robinson is not suggesting here that there has been bad faith by the State’s attorneys with

respect to discovery prosecution, but that is not the standard.

before a preliminary hearing. Cf. Opp. at 7. Mr. Robinson does argue that the State must disclose

evidence the defense has requested since its initial discovery request in October of 2025, that is

believes is necessary to render effective assistance of counsel and to ensure Mr. Robinson a

constitutionally rigorous and reliable preliminary hearing. See State v. Thomas, 1999 UT 2, ¶ 24,

or mitigates the offense.’” (additional citation omitted)). Whether these disclosures are what the
