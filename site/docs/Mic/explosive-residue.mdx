If a [high explosive](./high-explosive) functioned on Charlie Kirk's chest, the single most decisive
question is not what anyone saw — it is what stayed on the cloth. Post-blast residue is not fragile
trace evidence that vanishes in an afternoon. It transfers readily, it lodges in synthetic fiber, and
it comes back out under solvent extraction at concentrations laboratories routinely quantify.

## The claim, as posted

According to [@jonaaronbray](https://x.com/jonaaronbray/status/2083042911617200350) on X, July 31,
2026, summarizing the published literature:

> "Residues of military/high explosives (e.g., RDX, PETN, TNT-related compounds) have been detected
> on fabric after detonations or simulated post-blast conditions. One HPLC study recovered RDX at low
> concentrations (around 3–4 μg/g) from nylon and cotton cloth exposed in post-blast scenarios;
> recovery varied strongly by fabric type (better from nylon than cotton; none from denim in that
> work)."

That last clause matters as much as the headline. Recovery is **fabric-dependent**, and the study he
cites recovered nothing at all from denim. Any argument built on residue testing has to say which
garment, made of what.

## What the graphic says

The infographic posted with that text breaks the question into the three stages a forensic chemist
would separate. Its own text, as posted:

* **Transfer — rated HIGH.** "Handling or proximity to detonation readily transfers particles to
  synthetic surfaces. Fingerprint-level and post-blast residues common."
* **Trapping in fibers — rated HIGH.** "Nylon, polyester, and auto carpet (typically nylon or
  polyester blends) are porous and fibrous — particles lodge between fibers and in the pile. Better
  retention than many smooth surfaces."
* **Testability / recovery — rated MEDIUM-HIGH.** "Solvent extraction (acetone/methanol) + HPLC-UV
  or LC-MS recovers residues effectively. Nylon often shows higher recovery than cotton;
  polyester/auto carpet also workable. Detection at low μg/g to ng levels possible when properly
  sampled."

The ranking strip along the bottom runs **nylon (high recovery) → polyester / auto carpet (good–high)
→ cotton (lower)**. The graphic's own footer carries the caveat: it is "based on forensic studies of
post-blast and handling residues (RDX, PETN, TNT-related compounds)," and "actual results depend on
amount deposited, time, weathering, washing, and extraction method. Outdoor or heavily soiled carpet
reduces likelihood."

The inclusion of **auto carpet** is not incidental to this investigation. Kirk was carried into a
transport SUV and driven from the venue. Vehicle floor covering is a deep synthetic pile — by the
chart's own ranking, one of the better substrates for trapping and later recovering particulate.
The interior of that vehicle is the subject of the [SUV photos](./suv-transporting-charlie) page,
the [burnt object](./burnt-object-rode-transmitter) page, and the
[custody question](./Explosive_Residue_Coverup/fbi-didnt-take-car-custody) page. According to those
accounts the vehicle was towed, cleaned, and placed on a path to resale rather than held as a
long-term forensic shell. **No residue result from that vehicle has been made public.**

The same logic runs through the rest of the [Removing Explosive Residue Cover-Up](./Explosive_Residue_Coverup/overview)
section: the security detail's clothing, reportedly [seized by federal agents](./Explosive_Residue_Coverup/fbi-seized-security-team-clothing);
the [soil under the tent](./Explosive_Residue_Coverup/dirt-hauled-immediately), stripped and then
[sealed under pavers](./Explosive_Residue_Coverup/dirt-replaced-with-cement); the
[necklace](./Explosive_Residue_Coverup/charlie-kirk-necklace). Each is an ordinary step taken alone.
The question raised is about the set, and about the absence of a single released test result.

## What a test would actually settle

The method is not experimental. Swabs or fabric cuttings go into an acetone or methanol extraction;
the extract runs on **HPLC-UV** or **LC-MS** against a reference library of known explosive
compounds. The result is close to binary. A positive across the seized clothing, the shirt, the
vehicle carpet or the soil would confirm what investigators have concluded and reframe the entire case. The test is cheap, standard at any federal laboratory, and — on the
public record as it stands — has not been reported either way.

## What is not established

- **No explosive residue test result has been publicly released** for any item connected to
  September 10, 2025.
- **The recovery figures above come from published post-blast studies, not from any item in this
  case.** The 3–4 μg/g RDX figure describes laboratory and simulated post-blast cloth, not Kirk's
  shirt.
- **Collecting clothing and towing a vehicle are normal homicide procedure.** Nothing here
  establishes wrongdoing by any FBI employee, by any member of the security detail, by
  TPUSA, or by any other named person or organization. Nothing indicates anyone on
  the security or AV team knew of any device.
- **The government's narrative is a rifle gunshot wound.** Most citizen investigators have concluded
  the cause was an explosive at the microphone; this page documents why the residue test matters.
  See [the Mic hub](/Mic/overview).

If those fragments were recovered, they would be the most direct residue-test candidates of all. Video and write-up: *Black Shards Falling From Charlie Kirk's Shirt*.
