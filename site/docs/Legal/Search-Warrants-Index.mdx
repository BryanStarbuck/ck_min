**Twenty-four search warrant affidavits** have been filed and made public in *State of Utah v. Tyler Robinson*. Attorney and legal commentator Andrea Burkhart obtained the affidavits and published them to her Substack archive; the researcher [@Piper_truth1](https://x.com/Piper_truth1/status/2090143827671601438) then indexed them into the single reference table shown here — each warrant by **number**, with a one-line summary of what it sought.

The value of the index is that it shows the **shape of the investigation** in one view. What investigators asked a judge for, and in what order, is a record of what they believed they needed. Tyler Robinson is **charged, not convicted, and is presumed innocent**; nothing on this page is a finding of guilt against him or against any other person named in a warrant. A warrant is a request to search, granted on probable cause — it is not evidence that anything was found.

## What the Pattern Shows

**Digital evidence dominates.** Of the 24 warrants, roughly **seventeen** target phones, cloud accounts, carrier records, a chat platform, an ISP, a dash camera, or a car's infotainment system. Only a handful — the body and personal effects, the trace-evidence warrant, the residence, the apartment, the vehicle — are conventional physical searches. This is consistent with what the [Robinson phone and devices record](/court/Robinson_Phone_Devices) describes, and with prosecutors' reported position on what they intend to offer at trial.

**The same device was warranted more than once.** The dark-blue iPhone 13 appears twice — warrant 3213811 for the *contents* of the device and 3214291 for a *device search*. A second warrant against the same phone usually means the first authority was thought too narrow, or expired, or that a new probable-cause theory was needed to go further into the same handset. Which of those it was is not answerable from an index alone; it is answerable from the affidavits themselves.

**Three separate Verizon warrants and two Google warrants.** Warrants 3214341, 3214411, and 3214731 hit Verizon for account records, then location data for one number, then location data for a *second* number. Two numbers were being tracked. Alongside the cell-tower dump (3212941) and the anonymized geofence request to Google (3212501), the location-data effort here is substantial and was still expanding after the earliest warrants were served.

**The first warrant is for Charlie Kirk's body.** Warrant 3212261 covers Charlie Kirk's body and personal effects at Timpanogos Regional Hospital, and 3212321 covers hospital surveillance footage. Those two sit at the front of the sequence. Whatever was recorded under them is the material that would bear directly on the [cause-of-death question](/Cause_of_Death/overview) — the question the independent analysis says the rifle account settles too quickly, and much of the underlying record remains restricted under the [2026 evidence sealing](./Evidence-Sealing-2026).

## What the Index Does Not Tell You

An index of warrants is not the warrants. Reading the affidavits themselves is what would answer:

* **What probable cause was sworn** for each one, and by which affiant.
* **What was actually seized** under each warrant, and what returns were filed.
* **Whether any warrant was denied, narrowed, or later challenged** — a returned-and-refiled warrant looks identical to a first-time warrant in a number list.
* **Why the numbers are not contiguous.** The sequence runs 3212261 to 3217131 with wide gaps. Those gaps are almost certainly other Utah district warrants unrelated to this case rather than missing Robinson warrants — but "almost certainly" is an inference, not a verified fact, and only the court's own index settles it.
* **Whether 24 is the complete set.** Sealed warrants would not appear in a public archive at all. Given the [sealing posture in this case](./Evidence-Sealing-2026), a complete public list should not be assumed.

Two of the listed warrants name people other than the defendant. Warrant 3212881 lists an electronic device belonging to **Omar Najera**, and 3215171 lists **George Zinn's** phone in connection with CSAM evidence. Being named in a search warrant is not an accusation of involvement in Charlie Kirk's death, and neither listing should be read as one — Zinn was detained and cleared the same day as the shooter, and the matter attached to his phone in this warrant is a separate one from the assassination.

## Why This Belongs in the Disclosure Fight

A public, numbered index is exactly the artifact that makes a **forced-disclosure law** enforceable. Without it, "release the warrants" is an unbounded request that an agency can answer with a partial production and no one can audit. With it, every warrant has a number, and a missing number is a visible gap. The [Charlie Kirk Investigation Laws](/Fix/overview) turn on precisely this: disclosure obligations that can be checked against a list, rather than trust in a voluntary release.

That this index exists because a private attorney obtained the documents and a private researcher tabulated them — rather than because a court or agency published it — is itself part of the record on [how much of this case has stayed out of public view](/Legal/DOJ_Prosecution_And_Secrecy).
