## What the segment actually displays

From the video, 5 August 2026 — the speaker's own description of what is on his screen:

* Search results **split by language** — English, Yiddish and **Hebrew** — with the Hebrew entries
  singled out. His stated reasoning: *"There's only one nation that speaks Hebrew."*
* A Hebrew-language search for **"Erika Lane Frantzve"** — Erika Kirk's maiden
  name. He renders it on-air several ways; the speech transcript garbles it as "Erica Franzfay."
* Two dates named directly: **11 July 2025** and **24 July 2025**, the latter tied to **Omaha,
  Nebraska** — which he says is where an **Egyptian-registered military aircraft** was on the ground
  at that time.
* An **image search** he reads as **"Erika Lane Frantzve Scottsdale"**, which he says he chose
  because it is more specific than the name alone.
* Further Hebrew-language searches he places in the same windows: Tyler
  Robinson's **home address including the apartment number**, the address
  of the **parking lot where the white SUV was circling**, the **Losee Center**
  address, **area traffic**, and **preloaded U.S. SIM cards for Orem, Utah**.

Every date he names falls **before 10 September 2025**.

## His argument, in his words

The segment's reasoning is an analogy, and he states it plainly:

> "Our Egyptian military aircraft, again, they arrive and leave on a predictable timeline, just the
> same way that a cruise ship pulls into port… During that week, it would make sense if you saw a
> bunch of English searches taking place in Puerto Vallarta, because you know that the passengers
> there, they speak English. And so of course, they are Googling things on their phones. It's the
> same idea."

He is explicit that the search activity itself is unremarkable and that the language is the whole
argument:

> "This is not any kind of unusual behavior. It's the fact that it is being conducted in Hebrew,
> which is pretty specific to Israel."

And his conclusion:

> "We now have irrefutable banks of data that cannot be explained away. Egyptian military aircraft
> are on the ground. They're in the interior of our country when they're on the ground, from the
> location where they are at, Google searches, Google Image searches, web searches for all of the
> people closest to Charlie… including Tyler James Robinson, his exact home address, including his
> apartment number, the address to the parking lot where the white SUV was circling around, the
> address to the Losee building. The traffic in the area. SIM cards for Orem, Utah, U.S. SIM cards
> preloaded, all being done in Hebrew, at the exact time from the exact location where these
> Egyptian military aircraft are on the ground."

*(Transcript is machine-generated. "Mazad" is Mossad, "low seat building" is the Losee Center,
"Erica Franzfay" / "Erica Lane Franz Faye" are renderings of Erika Lane Frantzve. The full raw
transcript is preserved in this investigation's records.)*

**The earliest dated entry in this family is April 29, 2025, in Lincoln, Nebraska** — Tyler Robinson's apartment address, in Hebrew, from a segment the day before this one. That page identifies the jet (SU-BTU, confirmed at Lincoln 24–30 April by four flight sources) and reproduces the Google Trends settings shown on air: "Egyptian Jet" in Lincoln: Tyler Robinson's Address Searched in Hebrew.

## Being searched for is not evidence

This is the single most important limit on the page and it applies before any of the others.

A search **for** "Erika Lane Frantzve" makes her the **object** of somebody else's query. It carries
no implication that she knew of it, consented to it, arranged it, or had any connection whatsoever to
whoever ran it. On its face the same data is equally consistent with a widow being looked up by
strangers — which, in the weeks around and after her husband's assassination, is what the ordinary
reading would be.

## What the data cannot do

The standing caveat on every page in this section applies here too, and harder:

* **Trends reports interest in a term.** It does not identify who searched, from what device, from
  what account, or why. See [Israel IP search patterns](./israel-ip-search-patterns) for the same
  limitation stated at length.
* **Language is not nationality.** Hebrew-language search interest in a US metro does not establish
  that the searcher arrived on any particular aircraft, or arrived at all. Hebrew is read by a
  substantial US population.
* **Co-location in time is correlation.** The segment offers **no passenger manifest, no device
  attribution, and no subscriber record** tying a search to an aircraft. Two facts sharing a week and
  a metro area is where this argument starts, not where it ends.
* **Normalized, not counted.** Trends plots relative interest, so a visible Hebrew "spike" can rest
  on a very small number of queries.
* **The specific strings are unverified.** The claimed searches for an apartment number, a parking
  lot address and preloaded SIM cards are read off-screen in the segment; no archived, timestamped,
  reproducible pull of those exact terms has been published.

## Why it is kept anyway

The section's method is to record the claim and its limits rather than to drop it. Two things make
this one worth a page of its own:

1. It is **testable in principle**. Trends pulls are reproducible — the [Nester re-pull](./nester-israel-trends-repull)
   is the one claim in this section that has been reproduced on demand, and the same could be
   attempted here.
2. It sits at the junction of two independent threads — the search work in this section and the
   aircraft ground times worked out from ADS-B data. If the
   aircraft dates are wrong, the argument collapses without needing to touch the search side at all.

The video is mirrored here because material like this has been removed before —
preserving it is not endorsing it.
