## The post

On **August 13, 2026**, the account **[@ArtifexMemor](https://x.com/ArtifexMemor)** posted a Google Trends screenshot
with a two-line caption, quoting a poll from **[@DiligentDenizen](https://x.com/DiligentDenizen)**:

> its not every time
>
> but its every time

The line is deliberately compressed, and the poster does not spell out the argument. Read against the screenshot, the
claim being made is a claim about **repeatability**: that anyone who re-runs the same Google Trends query — the name
**Kathryn Nester**, geography restricted to **Israel** — gets the same flat line with the same
lone spike, and that the result therefore is not a one-off screenshot someone happened to catch at a favorable moment.

## What the screenshot actually shows

Taken on its own terms, and reading only what is visible in the image:

- The search term is **Kathryn Nester**, entered as a **search term** rather than as a topic or entity.
- The geography filter reads **Israel**. The time filter reads **Past 12 months**. Categories are **All categories**.
- The "Interest over time" line is **flat at or near zero** for the entire visible window except for a **single narrow
  spike reaching 100** — the maximum value on the normalized scale.
- The visible x-axis labels are **Oct 13, 2024** on the left and **Feb 2, 2025** at the midpoint, which places the spike
  in **late 2024**, consistent with the December 2024 window described on the existing
  [Kathryn Nester](./kathryn-nester-searches) page.

Nothing further can be read off the image. The right edge of the chart is cropped, so the end of the window is not
visible, and no absolute search volume is shown because **Google Trends never displays absolute volume**.

## Why the "every time" framing matters — and where it stops

The repeatability argument is a real methodological point, and it is worth stating fairly before stating its limits.

One of the standard objections to Trends screenshots is that they are **cherry-picked** — that a researcher tried many
name-and-geography combinations, kept the one that produced a dramatic line, and discarded the rest. A screenshot that
**reproduces on demand** answers that particular objection. If the same query returns the same shape whenever it is run,
the shape is at least a stable property of Google's data rather than an artifact of when the button was pressed.

What repeatability does **not** do is close any of the larger gaps, and those gaps are the ones that decide whether this
means anything:

- **Trends does not report IP addresses.** It reports *normalized relative interest* by inferred region. A reproducible
  Israel-geography line is not a reproducible list of Israeli searchers, and it identifies no individual.
- **A spike to 100 can be a very small number of searches.** The scale is relative to the peak within the selected
  window. For a name with almost no baseline traffic in a small country, a handful of queries — or a single
  news-driven cluster — can produce a line that looks identical to a coordinated one.
- **Reproducibility is expected, not surprising.** Trends returns sampled data, but the sample for a given query and
  window is generally stable across pulls. Getting the same chart twice is the normal behavior of the tool, not
  independent corroboration.
- **The spike still has no established cause.** Nothing in the screenshot connects the late-2024 activity to the
  September 10, 2025 shooting, to any person, or to any motive.

Put plainly: the post moves the claim from *"here is a screenshot"* to *"here is a screenshot you can reproduce."* That
is a modest improvement in evidentiary hygiene. It is not a bridge to attribution.

## The quoted poll

The screenshot was posted as a quote of a poll by **@DiligentDenizen** dated **August 12, 2026**, which asked followers:

> Do you think that federal government career employee public defender Kathrine Nester is giving Tyler Robinson a fair
> defense? 🤔
>
> More votes.

Two things should be said about that framing.

First, it is an **opinion poll of social-media followers**, not a finding. Its results measure what a self-selected
audience believes, and carry no evidentiary weight regarding the conduct of any attorney.

Second, the description in the poll is a characterization by its author. **Kathryn Nester** previously served as the
**Federal Public Defender for the District of Utah** and has practiced in private capital defense; she was appointed as
**capital defense counsel** for **Tyler Robinson** through the court's appointment process.
Whether that background is fairly summarized as "federal government career employee public defender" is the poll
author's editorial choice, not an established description used by this site.

## Defamation note

**Kathryn Nester** is a **living attorney** with an active professional obligation to her client. This page records a
**search-pattern claim** and a **social-media opinion poll**, both attributed to their authors. It makes **no
allegation** that she participated in, had foreknowledge of, or concealed anything relating to the death of
Charlie Kirk, and **no allegation** of professional misconduct or of an inadequate defense.
Court-appointed capital defense counsel are legally required to defend their client vigorously, and criticism of a
defense lawyer for doing so is criticism of the adversarial system, not evidence of wrongdoing.

**Tyler Robinson** has been **charged, not convicted**. Nothing on this page should be read as a statement of his guilt
or innocence.
