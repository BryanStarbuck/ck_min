Completing the record of the Tyler Robinson preliminary hearing

I got the transcripts to find out what we at home didn't get to hear that those present in the courtroom did.

Andrea Burkhart
Jul 17, 2026

As the preliminary hearing unfolded over the course of the last week, the court’s record on transparency for those of us watching from home deteriorated from not great to truly incomprehensible. We knew from the start that our ability to see and evaluate the exhibits presented at the hearing would be limited, because Judge Graf decided not to allow exhibits to be publicly available for inspection or copying and because the prosecution team hinted strongly in advance that they would not publish every exhibit admitted in the hearing to the courtroom, instead relying on the judge viewing it privately before deciding on whether probable cause was established.

But by day 4 of the hearing, we started having not only exhibits published inside the courtroom for the public attendees that the cameras weren’t allowed to capture, but also apparent testimony and arguments held in open court that the cameras were ordered to mute from the public broadcast. We never got any clear explanation for why those of us who tuned into the courtroom livestream from a respectful distance got the second-class citizen treatment compared to those in the courtroom.

The dumbest thing about the camera micromanagement by the court is that, since the entire process was held in open court, the record of everything that transpired is also public. So I ordered the transcript for days 4 and 5 - the days when the court decided to mute portions of the livestream - so that we could see for ourselves what happened just the same as the people who watched from inside the courtroom.

The mutings:

Day 4, during the testimony of SBI Agent Brian Davis:

Judge Graf muted the cameras for the reading of the note that Lance Twiggs testified Tyler Robinson left under the laptop, the full contents of which were already reported publicly when several of the search warrants were unsealed in April:

Day 4, also during the testimony of SBI Agent Brian Davis:

In this section, Judge Graf muted the cameras as soon as the prosecutor said “Discord.” The muting continued past the brief questioning about Tyler’s statements on Discord and through the argument about the admissibility of the affidavit of David Engelhardt, which was ultimately admitted and published to both the courtroom and the cameras (although the quality of the camera feed did not allow the home viewer to read what the affidavit said):

Day 4, during the testimony of SBI Sergeant Jennifer Faumuina:

Judge Graf muted testimony about the side-by-side comparison of the photograph of Tyler’s note when it was intact and photographed by Lance, to when it was found burnt in the trash during a search of the home, as well as argument about the admissibility of Faumuina’s testimony about the comparison:

There was also a brief part of day 4 that was lost when the stream buffered. We picked up the feed from Court TV and, as it turns out, little was lost.

On day 5, we had a portion of the feed muted after Judge Graf ruled that exhibits admitted during this day would not be shown on the camera. The camera operator apparently interpreted this order to preclude testimony about the exhibits as well, so we lost a few minutes of testimony about the DNA testing conducted by the ATF:

Overall, the only new information that wasn’t previously known publicly is:

  • Tyler mentioned the surveillance photos released on September 11th in the FBI bulletin in his Discord chat; and

  • The letters “E-D” and “R” may be visible on the burnt note in a position that corresponds with the same letters on the photograph of the note.

This exercise in recreation illustrates the absurdity of Judge Graf’s micromanagement of the camera feed and I hope it will not be repeated at future hearings. The muting of the audio feed is futile - if the hearing isn’t closed, then we will get to see what it says anyway - and does nothing but introduce friction into the public’s access by way of annoyance and expense to fill in the gaps. All of this broken trust for so little real utility - was it worth it, Judge?
